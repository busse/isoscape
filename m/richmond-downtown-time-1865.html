How this view was made

Downtown Richmond, from the Capitol to Tobacco Row, 1865. A frame 3 km across and 2 km deep (6.00 km²), centered on 37.5340°N, 77.4268°W.

The year 1865: After the Evacuation Fire how time is drawn →

Retreating Confederates fire the warehouses on 2–3 April; some 35 blocks of the business district burn.

Measured: 196 buildings standing today that had been built by 1865. Their years: 96 from the city assessor’s year built, 90 from OpenStreetMap’s start_date, 10 curated. Anything demolished since is missing unless traced or curated below.

Streets: a street is drawn only where a building of the year stood within reach of it or the town plan laid it out: 2,454 of today’s are left off. Whatever the era rules do not date (parks, cemeteries, dams, piers, parking aisles) is drawn in today’s scene only.

Curated for this year, from published sources; positions are drawn from today’s street grid and published dimensions, not surveyed:

  • Great Turning Basin, James River & Kanawha Canal (1800–1880). The canal's Great Basin covered three blocks between Cary and Canal Streets, from 8th to 12th Street. Completed about 1800; railroad yards covered about half of it in the 1880s and all of it by the 1920s (the Kanawha Plaza marker). Drawn whole until 1880 (that year approximate); for what was left of it in 1886 see the next entries. Drawn as still water at its own level, with a smooth bank, on today's ground (see How it's made). The James Center stands on the site. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]
  • James River and Kanawha Canal, from Tredegar to the Great Basin (1800–1895). The canal's last mile into the Great Basin. Its middle stretch, between 6th and 7th Streets, is read from the 1886 Sanborn map (volume 1, sheet 10, "Old James River & Kanawha Canal (Mill Canal)"), placed by the corners of 6th and Byrd, 7th and Byrd and 7th and Canal. West of it the line runs straight to the east end of the canal's dry bed by Tredegar mapped in OpenStreetMap (w307593380), and east of it straight to the basin's west corner, as the 1886 key map shows it: those two runs are approximate. Drawn about 10 m wide (sheet 10 draws it about 8 m) from 1800, the basin's year, to 1895, the end of the basin entries; the railroad laid on its towpath from 1880 is not drawn. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]
  • Burned district, Evacuation Fire of 3 April 1865 (1865–1866). Some 35 blocks burned as the Confederate government left the city, from 4th Street east to 16th Street and from the river north, in places, as far as Capitol Square. Drawn simplified: 4th to 16th Streets, from midway between Main and Franklin Streets to the river. The mills and warehouses along the canal burned with it. [1] [2]
  • Libby Prison (warehouses, 1845–52; prison 1862–65) (1845–1889). Three brick warehouses, each 110 × 44 ft and four stories, on the east side of 20th Street between Cary and Dock. Used as a Confederate prison 1862–65; dismantled in 1889 and re-erected in Chicago. Footprint from the published dimensions, placed at the corner; not surveyed. [1]
  • Gallego Mills, the great mill of 1854 (1855–1866). Twelve stories, 96 ft on Canal Street and 165 ft deep, at the southwest corner of 12th and Canal: by 1860 the largest building in Richmond and claimed as the largest flour mill in the world. Burned in the Evacuation Fire. Footprint from the published dimensions; position from the marker; height assumes about 3.3 m a story. [1] [2] [3]
  • Lumpkin's Jail (slave-trading complex) (1835–1876). The largest of Richmond's slave jails, in the slave-trading district of Shockoe Bottom: a two-and-a-half-story brick building about 41 ft long, operated from the 1830s until 1865 and demolished by 1876. Its foundations were excavated in 2008 beside Main Street Station. Footprint approximate; position from the historical marker. [1] [2]
  • Shockoe Creek (open stream) (1737–1890). The creek ran open between present-day 15th and 16th Streets, dividing the town. A culvert begun in 1877 enclosed part of it, but the 1886 Sanborn map (volume 1, key map and sheets 18 and 19) still draws it open from Broad Street to the Dock, so it is drawn to 1890 (the end approximate). Course approximated as midway between the two streets. [1] [2]
  • Richmond and Petersburg (Atlantic Coast Line) approach to Byrd Street Station (1838–1971). The track from the north end of the Richmond and Petersburg bridge (OSM w710689016) across the Haxall Canal into Byrd Street Station at Seventh and Byrd Streets, the R&P's original Richmond station and later the Atlantic Coast Line's headquarters, closed in 1958. Today nothing of it is mapped north of the bridge. Approximate: one track along the middle of the station's throat, read by eye from the USGS 1:24,000 Richmond quadrangles of 1934 and 1956 (which show the line crossing the canal and fanning into the station yard between Seventh and Eighth Streets south of Byrd) and joined to the bridge's mapped end, about 20 m east of where the maps put it. Drawn with the bridge, from 1838 to 1970 (the R&P's depot stood at 8th and Byrd before Byrd Street Station, and the 1886 Sanborn map, volume 1, sheet 10, shows its tracks fanning in between 7th and 9th south of Canal); the years are the bridge's, not the track's. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

The ground is today’s: cuts and fills since (the interstate, the floodwall) are in the elevation.

Camera how the camera works →

Looking north (bearing 351°), a fixed bearing with no snapping. Orthographic, tilted 30° down from the horizon: ground running into the picture is drawn at 0.5× its length and heights at 0.87×. 2 px per meter across the frame; the whole drawing is 6,048 × 2,642 px at 1:1.

Sources where the data comes from →

  • OpenStreetMap: 1,068 features read from the cached download, shared with region richmond-i195-rocketts.
  • USGS 3DEP elevation (United States (U.S. Geological Survey); bare-earth lidar, 1 m across most of the US, served by the National Map), fetched at 2 m per pixel and resampled to a 5 m grid. The ground runs from -0.1 m to 56.8 m above sea level (56.9 m of relief), drawn 2.5× taller than life. Buildings and bridges keep their true heights.

Ground what the ground is made of →

From the map, 34 mapped areas of water, islands, parks, woods and other cover, and 432 ribbons of road, path, rail, canal and weir. Painted in 200 strips from back to front, 10 m deep each, so hills hide what is behind them (why). Contours every 2 m with every 5th line heavier: 48,689 segments.

Rivers and rapids how rapids are read →

9,125 grid cells (about 22.8 ha) read as rapids: places on a river where its surface, recovered from the lidar, falls faster than 4 m per km along its channel, measured over 200 m. Rock standing more than 1 m clear of the water around it is drawn as boulders; lakes, basins and the sea get neither. Both are inferred from the lidar, not observed.
