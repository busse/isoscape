# Graded: Richmond's 1937 map and the ground since

> **Rough draft, for Chris's review.** This page is not listed on the site. It holds the first
> pictures and a skeleton of the text. Facts marked *to verify* come from the research brief and
> were not reopened for this draft. Nobody outside has read it, and the people whose work it
> rests on have not been asked. Drafted with Claude for Chris Busse, {{date}}.

In 1937 the federal Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) graded Richmond's residential areas
from A, "Best", to D, "Hazardous". Chris Busse has drawn those grades with isoscape on the
ground they were given to, with the buildings standing then, and the same ground today with the
highways built across it since. This page goes in order of time and ends with
[how we know](#how-we-know) what each picture shows and where it may mislead.

The map recorded a city already divided by its own laws and by private practice, and the grades
it gave line up with much that followed. Whether the map itself caused what followed is argued
among historians and economists, and this page sets out both sides
([What the map did](#what-the-map-did)).

## Before the map

Richmond drew its lines long before 1937. In 1871 the city's wards were redrawn to hold as many
Black voters as possible in one ward, Jackson Ward [[LVA timeline](#sources)]. From 1888 electric
streetcars opened suburbs that deeds and covenants kept white [[LVA timeline](#sources)]. In April
1911 City Council adopted a residential segregation ordinance, and in 1912 the General Assembly
let every Virginia city do the same [[LVA timeline](#sources)]. The Supreme Court struck such
ordinances down in *Buchanan v. Warley* in 1917. Richmond then passed a new one tied to the
state's racial integrity law: the Library of Virginia dates it 1929, Encyclopedia Virginia 1924,
and both say a federal appeals court struck it down, in 1929 by Encyclopedia Virginia's account
[[LVA timeline](#sources); [Gray-Sparks](#sources)]. Covenants stayed enforceable until *Shelley v.
Kraemer* in 1948 [[LVA timeline](#sources)].

The map is titled "Richmond, Va. Security Map" and is held by the National Archives with 48
written area descriptions [[NARA RG 195](#sources); [Mapping Inequality](#sources)]. As Mapping
Inequality digitized it, it grades {{A_n}} areas A, {{B_n}} B, {{C_n}} C and {{D_n}} D, and one
more area is marked "Industrial and Commercial" and left ungraded
[[numbers](#how-we-know)].

::: figure
![A bar chart of graded land by grade: A {{A_km2}} square kilometers, B {{B_km2}}, C {{C_km2}}, D {{D_km2}}](redlining-land.webp)

Figure 2. Graded land by grade, measured from the digitized map.
:::

D was {{D_pct}}% of the graded land, the largest of the four grades by area. The descriptions
were written on a standard form by local real estate people working for the HOLC (*to verify*
for Richmond). The surveyors wrote of Monument Avenue and the Fan (C2), graded C, "The peak has
passed" [[HOLC area description C2](#sources)]. For D1, the core of Jackson Ward, the form
records a population 95% Black, 20% home ownership and family incomes of $350 to $750 a year
[[HOLC area description D1](#sources)]. The forms' language about race is quoted here only in
summary.

## What the map did

Two lines of research read the same maps differently, and this story does not choose between them.

- **Lasting effects.** Aaronson, Hartley and Mazumder compare neighborhoods on either side of
  the maps' grade boundaries and find that the maps "led to reduced home ownership rates, house
  values, and rents and increased racial segregation in later decades", which they attribute to
  reduced access to credit [[Aaronson et al. 2021](#sources)].
- **A record of what was already there.** Fishback, LaVoice, Shertzer and Walsh find that racial
  bias in drawing the maps explains at most 4 to 20 percent of the concentration of Black
  households in D zones; most lived there because of earlier disadvantage and discrimination
  [[Fishback et al. 2023](#sources)]. Fishback, Rose, Snowden and Storrs find that the Federal
  Housing Administration's exclusion of low-income urban neighborhoods came before the HOLC
  maps, and that the HOLC itself "broadly loaned" to such neighborhoods and to Black homeowners
  [[Fishback et al. 2022](#sources)].

The HOLC made nearly all its loans before the maps were drawn (*to verify*: Michney, 2023).
Whether the HOLC or FHA lent in Richmond's own D areas has not been studied, as far as the brief
found.

Figure 3. A5 (Windsor Farms) and D1 (Jackson Ward and Gilpin), the same ground width
(2.6 km), in 1937 and today. *How this was made:* as figure 1; today, the buildings and streets

A5 was laid out in the 1920s on the bluffs above the James (*to verify*). In 1937 it was still
filling in; today it is built out. D1 in 1937 shows the blocks the Sanborn sheets traced, dense
from Jackson Ward north to Apostle Town. Today the Richmond–Petersburg Turnpike, I-95 and I-64,
crosses it.

## What was built on D land, 1941 to 1977

::: compare wide
![Richmond in 1937 with the HOLC's grades on the ground](redlining-grades-1937.webp) 1937
![Richmond today with the same grades under it and the limited-access highways in black](redlining-built-2026.webp) today
:::

Figure 4. The same frame in 1937 and today. Drag the divider. Today's limited-access highways
are drawn in black over the 1937 grades.

- **Gilpin Court.** The Richmond Housing Authority's first clearance was Apostle Town in D1.
  Gilpin Court, 300 units, replaced it and displaced 250 Black families
  [[Gray-Sparks](#sources)]. It opened in 1943 (*to verify*).
- **The Turnpike.** By 1957, the *Richmond Times-Dispatch* estimated, 1,900 Black families,
  about 10 percent of the city's Black population, had been displaced by the
  Richmond–Petersburg Turnpike and related construction [[Gray-Sparks](#sources)].
- **Mosby Court.** The 17th Street urban renewal project cleared 150 acres of Shockoe Valley,
  displacing 1,300 mostly Black residents; many went to Mosby Court, opened in 1962 in "a
  previously redlined neighborhood" [[Gray-Sparks](#sources)]. It stands in D5
  (*to verify* against the map).
- **Fulton.** Fulton Bottom (D7), about 850 homes, churches, schools and stores, was declared a
  slum in 1967; the 1970 renewal plan would displace about 785 households, and the last homes
  came down in the late 1980s [[Gray-Sparks](#sources)].
- **Randolph and the Downtown Expressway.** HUD approved the 380-acre Randolph project in 1973;
  its planning study expected about 1,800 families and individuals to be displaced, to clear land
  and make way for the Downtown Expressway [[Gray-Sparks](#sources)]. The expressway runs
  through D8.

The highway inside D land runs through three areas: D1 ({{hD1}} km), D8 ({{hD8}} km) and D5
({{hD5}} km). Most of the region's limited-access mileage lies outside the 1937 graded areas
altogether ({{hkm}} of {{hkm_all}} km in the cached area). Other public housing courts sit near but outside D land, and one stood on ground
the map left ungraded (*to verify*: Creighton, Fairfield, Whitcomb and Hillside Courts). The
routes were chosen by the Turnpike Authority, the city and the state; the Jackson Ward story
follows how the Turnpike's was chosen.

## 1970: annexation

In 1970 Richmond annexed 23 square miles and 47,000 people from Chesterfield County, 97% of them
white, and the city's Black share fell from 52% to 42%; the courts later let the city keep the
land on condition that it elect its council by wards [[LVA timeline](#sources)].

## How we know

- **The grades** are Mapping Inequality's digitization of the 1937 map, downloaded from the
  University of Richmond's Digital Scholarship Lab on November 21, 2026. They are drawn as
  published and not yet checked against the scan.
- **Buildings of 1937.** A dated picture draws buildings standing today that the city's assessor
  dates to 1937 or before, plus, in Jackson Ward, the buildings on the 1925 and 1952 Sanborn
  sheets. Buildings since lost elsewhere are missing. Where the 1952 sheets were traced, only
  {{jw_pct}}% of their {{jw_n}} outlines stand today [[numbers](#how-we-know)]. Across the
  graded land, the share of today's dated parcels built by 1937 runs from {{yA}}% in A to
  {{yC}}% in C, with D at {{yD}}%. So the pictures undercount 1937's buildings most where most
  was cleared, which is in D.
- **Areas and lengths** are measured by `tools/stories/redlining/measure.py` on a local plane.
  Each grade's land is its polygons merged. Highway length is OpenStreetMap's `motorway` ways,
  each carriageway counted, ramps left out; with ramps, D holds {{hlD}}%.
- **The frame** holds all of A and B, {{fD}}% of D and {{fC}}% of C; the east ends of C13 and
  C14 and the south tip of D11.5 fall outside it.
- **The ground** is 2010s lidar, three times exaggerated. In Jackson Ward the Turnpike's trench
  is filled for 1937 by the estimate the Jackson Ward story made.
