## In short

"Redlining" today means a lender's refusing credit because of the neighborhood a borrower lives
in, and the word comes from the red of the lowest grade on a set of federal maps
[[FRED 2021](#sources)]. In 1937 a federal agency, the Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC),
surveyed Richmond and graded its residential areas from A, "Best", to D, "Hazardous"
[[FRASER, Richmond](#sources)]. Its forms recorded ten of Richmond's twelve D areas as Black;
of the other two, one was vacant land and one was recorded as white
[[FRASER, Richmond area descriptions](#sources)]. Drawn on the ground and measured, the D land
[lies lowest](#the-whole-city-in-1937-the-four-grades-on-richmonds-terrain) of the four grades.
It lies nearest downtown, where the highways converge: D was {{D_pct}}% of the graded land and
holds {{hD}}% of the limited-access highway inside it, where location alone would give it about
{{exp_D}}% ([how this is measured](#where-the-highways-run-measured-against-a-fair-comparison)).
The Library of Virginia writes that the city's public housing was built, by and large, in D
areas [[LVA timeline](#sources)]. Whether the map itself caused any of this is disputed among
researchers; [three readings](#did-the-1937-map-cause-what-followed-three-readings) are set out
below, and the page does not choose between them. Terms are explained in the
[glossary](#glossary-of-terms-used-on-this-page).

## What the 1937 map was: a short guide for readers new to it

### The Home Owners' Loan Corporation and why Congress made it

In the Great Depression, Congress created the Home Owners' Loan Corporation (the HOLC) in the
Home Owners' Loan Act, signed on June 13, 1933, to refinance the mortgages of home owners in
distress [[Harriss 1951](#sources); [Rose 2022](#sources)]. Between August 1933 and June 1936 it
made over one million refinancing loans, 1,017,821 by Harriss's count, for $3.1 billion; it
stopped taking applications in June 1935 and made its last loans in June 1936
[[Harriss 1951](#sources)]. By an estimate from the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, it had
already made 90% of its loans before its mapping project began in 1935 [[Rose 2022](#sources)].
Price Fishback and coauthors conclude that the maps had virtually no influence on the HOLC's
own refinancing, and in 1940 Black home owners held 4.5% of its mortgages, about their
share of the country's nonfarm home owners [[Fishback et al. 2023](#sources)]. Studying
Philadelphia, Amy Hillier found that the HOLC made more than 60 percent of its loans there to
areas later colored red [[Hillier 2003](#sources)].

### The city survey and its four grades

From 1935 to 1940 the HOLC surveyed the residential areas of 239 American cities in its City
Survey Program and drew a color-coded map of each, rating how risky it was to make mortgage
loans in each neighborhood; the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis (the St. Louis Fed) writes
that the practice informed how the Federal
Home Loan Bank Board supervised lenders [[FRED 2021](#sources); [National Archives, RG 195](#sources);
[Hillier 2003](#sources)].
Whether and how lenders used the maps afterward is part of
[the dispute below](#did-the-1937-map-cause-what-followed-three-readings). Richmond's map is
titled "Residential Security Map" [[FRASER, Richmond](#sources)]. Each area got one of four
grades:

| Grade | On Richmond's map legend | The HOLC's name for it | Color on the map | What the grade was meant to say |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | First Grade | "Best" | green | values expected to rise or hold high |
| B | Second Grade | "Still Desirable" | blue | values expected to hold |
| C | Third Grade | "Definitely Declining" | yellow | values expected to fall |
| D | Fourth Grade | "Hazardous" | red | old housing, or near nuisances such as industry; in Richmond, 10 of the 12 D areas recorded as Black |

Richmond's map names its grades only by number: its legend reads "First Grade" to "Fourth
Grade" [[FRASER, Richmond](#sources)]. The names in quotation marks are the HOLC's too: they are
handwritten on the legend of its map of Atlanta, held in the same National Archives records
[[FRASER, Atlanta](#sources)], and the St. Louis Fed's summary uses them, with "Declining" for C
[[FRED 2021](#sources)]. This page uses both, with the names always in quotation marks as the
HOLC's words. The last column follows the St. Louis Fed's summary, which does not mention race
[[FRED 2021](#sources)]; its last clause is from Richmond's forms
[[FRASER, Richmond area descriptions](#sources)].

Richmond's map, prepared by the HOLC's Division of Research & Statistics, is dated
April 3, 1937. The HOLC drew it on the city's own base map, "City of Richmond, Virginia and Environs",
made by the Department of Public Works in 1923 and revised to August 1, 1936. It is held by the
National Archives in the records of the Federal Home Loan Bank Board, Record Group 195
[[FRASER, Richmond](#sources); [National Archives, RG 195](#sources)]. It grades {{A_n}} areas
A, {{B_n}} B, {{C_n}} C and {{D_n}} D [[counted on the map](#how-we-know-the-redrawn-map-the-buildings-and-the-measures)],
the same count the University of Richmond's Digital Scholarship Lab gives in *Mapping
Inequality*, the project that digitized and published these maps for the whole country
[[Mapping Inequality](#sources)]. The map numbers the areas within each grade, A1 to A7 and so
on, and one D area carries the number 11½, written D11.5 here.

### The area descriptions: one printed form for each area

For every area the surveyors filled in a printed form, an "area description". Its fields ask for
the terrain, what made the area more or less desirable, the residents' "type" (filled in with an
occupation, a race or a rank), family incomes, the share of foreign-born residents, the share of
Black residents (in a field headed "Negro"), any "infiltration" (on four of Richmond's D forms,
D2, D5, D6 and D7, the entry is "Negroes"), house types, ages and repair, prices and rents, how
many homes were lived in by their owners, and how much mortgage money was available
[[FRASER, Richmond area descriptions](#sources)]. The mortgage-money field records what the
person who supplied the form's information said local lenders were offering; by 1937 the HOLC
itself had stopped lending [[Harriss 1951](#sources)]. Richmond's forms name those people in
their last field: J. D. Carneal on D1 to D9, and A. L. Adamson, a South Side realtor, on D10,
D11 and D11.5 [[FRASER, Richmond area descriptions](#sources)]. Economists who have studied the
maps describe the forms as recording how local real estate professionals judged lending risk
[[Fishback et al. 2023](#sources)].

The forms record race throughout, and Richmond's use it to judge places and houses. The form for
C7, on the north side, faults its area for lying too near D2
[[FRASER, Richmond area descriptions](#sources), p. 29]. The form for C4 says its area is yellow
"largely because" its school for white children lies in D8 and because Black residents of D8
pass through it to reach William Byrd Park [[FRASER, Richmond area descriptions](#sources), p.
26, field 14].

*Content note: the next paragraph quotes a racial slur from the 1937 form.*

The form for D11.5 (Blackwell) rated the condition of its houses by the race of the people who
would live in them. So that readers can see the standard the forms applied, this page quotes that
field once, as the HOLC's form recorded it in 1937, and paraphrases the forms' racial terms
everywhere else. The repair field reads: "Bad for whites; Fair for darkies"
[[FRASER, Richmond area descriptions](#sources), p. 47, field 6d].

### Where the word "redlining" comes from

The St. Louis Fed writes that the word comes from the red the maps used for the lowest grade,
and that it also names discriminatory practices in lending [[FRED 2021](#sources)]. At
Northwestern University, John McKnight and colleagues investigated redlining, banks' systematic
denial of mortgages and other financial services to people in minority neighborhoods; the
university's Institute for Policy Research credits that work with laying the groundwork for two
federal laws [[Northwestern IPR 2024](#sources)]. The Home Mortgage Disclosure Act, enacted in
1975, requires many lenders to report and publicly disclose their mortgage lending
[[CFPB, HMDA](#sources)]. The Community Reinvestment Act, enacted in 1977, directs bank
regulators to encourage banks to meet the credit needs of the communities where they do
business, low- and moderate-income neighborhoods included [[Federal Reserve, CRA](#sources)].
What the HOLC's own map did is the subject of
[the dispute below](#did-the-1937-map-cause-what-followed-three-readings).

## Richmond's own racial lines before the map, 1871 to 1948

Richmond drew its lines long before 1937. In 1871 the city's wards were redrawn to create a
single Black-majority ward, Jackson Ward [[LVA timeline](#sources)]. From 1888 electric
streetcars opened suburbs, most of them kept white by deed restrictions [[LVA timeline](#sources)].
In April 1911 City Council adopted an ordinance segregating where white and Black residents
could live, and in 1912 the General Assembly let every Virginia city do the same
[[LVA timeline](#sources); [Gray-Sparks](#sources)]. The Supreme Court struck such ordinances
down in *Buchanan v. Warley* in 1917 [[LVA timeline](#sources)]. Richmond then passed a new
ordinance, based on Virginia's Racial Integrity Act, that barred residents from living in a
neighborhood where they could not marry a member of the majority population. Encyclopedia Virginia dates it 1924 and the
Library of Virginia 1929; a federal appeals court struck it down in 1929
[[Gray-Sparks](#sources); [LVA timeline](#sources)]. Racial covenants, clauses in deeds that
restricted who could buy or live in a house, stayed enforceable until *Shelley v. Kraemer* in
1948 [[LVA timeline](#sources)].

## One A area and one D area, form by form: A5, Windsor Farms, and D1 (Jackson Ward)

Before the whole city, here are two areas the HOLC graded at the two ends of its scale, chosen
for that reason: A5, Windsor Farms, in the West End beside the James, and D1 (Jackson Ward,
Gilpin, Carver and Navy Hill), north of downtown. A name after a comma is the one typed at the
head of the 1937 form; a name in parentheses is today's.

### A5, Windsor Farms: what the form recorded, and the ground

T. C. Williams, a tobacconist, founded Windsor Farms in 1926 on a 444-acre tract beside the
river, laid out by the planner John Nolen as an English village around a green
[[TCLF, Windsor Farms](#sources)]. Eleven years later the HOLC form for A5 recorded rolling
ground, hilly toward the river; residents whose "type" it gave as "Best people"; family incomes
of $7,500 and up; every home lived in by its owner; brick houses on 100-foot lots up to estates
of 10 and 15 acres, two to eight years old, in excellent repair; and mortgage money for buying a
home rated good. In the field for favorable influences it called A5 the most desirable area in
or about Richmond [[HOLC area description A5](#sources)].

### D1 (Jackson Ward, Gilpin, Carver and Navy Hill): what the form recorded, and the ground

Jackson Ward was then the center of Richmond's Black community, the Library of Virginia writes
[[LVA, House to Highway](#sources)], known as "Black Wall Street" and the "Harlem of the South"
for its Black-owned businesses and entertainment venues [[City of Richmond, Jackson Ward](#sources)].
D1 is drawn in several pieces north of downtown, from Jackson Ward through Apostle Town, the
neighborhood later cleared for Gilpin Court, to Navy Hill. Its form recorded rolling ground; a
population 95% Black; family incomes of $350 to $750 a year; one home in five lived in by its
owner; single houses, 40% brick and 60% frame, forty years old and more, in poor to very poor
repair; and mortgage money for buying a home, as the form's informant reported local lenders
offering it, at 60% of appraisal (read plainly, loans of up to 60% of a house's appraised value).
It wrote that the population was falling "because of demolition to save taxes"
[[HOLC area description D1](#sources)].

After 1937 the Richmond Housing Authority cleared Apostle Town and dedicated Gilpin Court,
public housing, there on April 28, 1943; a newspaper report that month counted 301 units,
Encyclopedia Virginia 300, and Encyclopedia Virginia says 250 Black families were displaced
[[Craig 2025](#sources); [Gray-Sparks](#sources)]. The Richmond–Petersburg Turnpike, a toll
expressway now part of I-95, was proposed in 1946 and defeated twice in referendums; the
General Assembly then created the Richmond–Petersburg Turnpike Authority, with the power of
eminent domain, which built the road through the ward in the 1950s. It opened on July 1, 1958
[[Gray-Sparks](#sources); [LVA timeline](#sources); [VCU Libraries](#sources)]. By 1957 the
*Richmond Times-Dispatch* estimated that about 1,900 Black families, about 10% of the city's
Black population, had been displaced by it and related work [[Gray-Sparks](#sources)].
Virginia Commonwealth University's (VCU's) medical campus now covers most of Navy Hill
[[Gray-Sparks](#sources)]. The [Jackson Ward story](jackson-ward.md) follows the Turnpike block
by block.

## What was built and cleared on graded land, 1943 to 1977

The 1937 grades, the housing sites and the highway routes may share causes. VCU Libraries writes
that planners and turnpike officials chose for the Turnpike's path areas they considered
"blighted" or home to many Black Richmonders [[VCU Libraries](#sources)], and Encyclopedia
Virginia that the city's 1946 master plan recommended demolishing Black neighborhoods it judged to
be deteriorating [[Gray-Sparks](#sources)]; the 1937 forms had recorded the same neighborhoods' race
and the age of their houses. The pictures and measures here show where things were built. Why
they were built there is taken up in
[the dispute below](#did-the-1937-map-cause-what-followed-three-readings).

::: compare wide
![Richmond in 1937 with the HOLC's grades on the ground](redlining-grades-1937.webp) 1937
![Richmond today from the same point, with the same grades under it and the limited-access highways drawn in black](redlining-built-2026.webp) today

Richmond in 1937 (left of the divider) and today (right), the same view, with the 1937 grades
under both. Today's limited-access highways, entered only by ramps, are drawn in black. *How
this was made:* as the 1937 figure; today, OpenStreetMap's buildings and streets, with its
highway and ramp categories for limited-access roads in black.

The Library of Virginia writes that, by and large, the city's public housing was built in
neighborhoods the HOLC had graded D [[LVA timeline](#sources)]. The sources say where each
project below is; this page's drawing says which area that is. The Richmond Housing Authority,
later the Richmond Redevelopment and Housing Authority (RRHA), built the public housing among them
[[Craig 2025](#sources); [Gray-Sparks](#sources)].

- **Gilpin Court, in D1, 1943**, on cleared Apostle Town, enlarged in 1957 for families the
  Turnpike displaced [[Craig 2025](#sources); [Gray-Sparks](#sources)].
- **The Richmond–Petersburg Turnpike, through D1, built in the 1950s and opened July 1, 1958**,
  now I-95 and I-64 [[LVA timeline](#sources); [VCU Libraries](#sources)]; about 1,900 Black
  families displaced by 1957 by the *Times-Dispatch*'s estimate [[Gray-Sparks](#sources)].
- **Fairfield and Whitcomb Courts, 1958**, opened for more of the families highway building
  displaced [[Gray-Sparks](#sources)]. Most of the addresses in their HUD records that
  OpenStreetMap places lie in D5 [[HUD via ProPublica](#sources);
  [measured here](#how-we-know-the-redrawn-map-the-buildings-and-the-measures)].
- **Mosby Court, in D5, 1962**: the City of Richmond's 17th Street Urban Renewal Project cleared
  150 acres of Shockoe Valley and displaced 1,300 mostly Black residents, many of whom moved to
  Mosby Court, which opened in 1962 in an isolated part of the East End [[Gray-Sparks](#sources)].
  HUD's record gives its address as 1909 Accommodation Street [[HUD via ProPublica](#sources)],
  and the whole of Accommodation Street lies inside D5
  [[measured here](#how-we-know-the-redrawn-map-the-buildings-and-the-measures)].
- **D7, Fulton, 1967 to the late 1980s**: in 1967 a study sponsored by the RRHA designated Fulton
  Bottom, a neighborhood of about 850 homes, churches, schools and stores on 200 acres, a "slum".
  The 1970 renewal plan would displace about 785 households, nearly 3,000 people. The RRHA began
  buying and razing Fulton properties in the early 1970s, and the last homes on Denny Street came
  down in the late 1980s [[Gray-Sparks](#sources)].
- **D11.5 (Blackwell), 1970**: 464 scattered-site public housing units were built in the
  neighborhood [[Eshleman 2018](#sources)].
- **D8 (Randolph) and the Downtown Expressway, 1973**: HUD approved the 380-acre Randolph Urban
  Renewal Project; its planning study expected about 1,800 families and individuals to be
  displaced, and Encyclopedia Virginia gives its aims as "slum clearance" and making way for the
  six-lane Downtown Expressway [[Gray-Sparks](#sources)]. The Library of Virginia writes that the
  expressway cut the Randolph and Oregon Hill neighborhoods in half [[LVA timeline](#sources)].

Not all of the city's public housing went to D land. Creighton Court and Hillside Court, which
the housing authority also built (no opened source dates them), stand on land the HOLC did not
grade, by nearly all of their HUD addresses that OpenStreetMap places, and Hillside Court served low-income white residents
[[Craig 2025](#sources); [HUD via ProPublica](#sources);
[measured here](#how-we-know-the-redrawn-map-the-buildings-and-the-measures)].

Half of the D land lies within {{med_D}} km of the State Capitol; for C the distance is
{{med_C}} km, for B {{med_B}} km and for A {{med_A}} km. So the fair comparison for the D land is
other land near the center. Per square kilometer, D land carries {{dens_D}} km of this highway,
against {{dens_ungraded}} km on the ungraded land in the citywide view (downtown, industry, rail
yards, parks, the river and land outside the 1937 survey) and {{dens_C}} km on C. Within
{{near_km}} km of the Capitol, where {{near_D_km2}} km² of the D land lies, D land carries
{{near_D}} km per square kilometer, against {{near_ungraded}} km on the ungraded land and

## Did the 1937 map cause what followed? Three readings

The plain question is this. Did the map cause later harm by steering money away from the areas
it marked red? Or did it record a city already divided, whose later history had other causes?
Researchers have read the same maps both ways. All three readings below find that the D areas
were already poorer and more heavily Black when the maps were drawn; they differ on how much the
HOLC's map itself added afterward. This page does not choose between them. Each is set out in
its authors' terms.

- **Lower-graded sides of the map lines fell behind for decades.** Daniel Aaronson, Daniel
  Hartley and Bhashkar Mazumder compare neighborhoods on either side of the maps' grade
  boundaries, nationally, with similar boundaries the maps did not draw, and find that the maps
  "led to reduced home ownership rates, house values, and rents and increased racial segregation
  in later decades", which they attribute to reduced access to credit and the disinvestment that
  followed [[Aaronson et al. 2021](#sources)]. They also report that the two sides of the lines
  already differed before the maps, and they weight their comparison boundaries to match those
  differences. Along the lines between D and C areas, the gap in the share of Black residents
  peaked at 15 to 17 percentage points between 1950 and 1970 and stood at about 3 points
  by 2010. They write that their estimates include any effect of the Federal Housing
  Administration (FHA) where its lines and the HOLC's agree [[Aaronson et al. 2021](#sources)],
  and a later paper by the same group treats such estimates as a proxy for the broader practice
  of redlining that arose largely through federal housing policy [[Aaronson et al. 2022](#sources)].
- **The maps recorded disadvantage that was already there.** Price Fishback, Jessica LaVoice,
  Allison Shertzer and Randall Walsh find that the maps show how real estate professionals judged
  lending risk by race, income and housing quality, and that racial bias in drawing the maps
  explains at most 4 to 20 percent of the concentration of Black households in D zones; they
  attribute most of it to earlier decades of disadvantage and discrimination
  [[Fishback et al. 2023](#sources)].
- **The other federal agency drew its own lines first.** Price Fishback, Jonathan Rose, Kenneth
  Snowden and Thomas Storrs find that the FHA, the federal agency that insured private lenders'
  mortgages, did not insure mortgages in low-income urban neighborhoods, where most urban Black
  Americans lived, from its start in the 1930s, before the HOLC maps, while the HOLC lent broadly
  in those neighborhoods and to Black home owners; they conclude the HOLC maps had little effect
  on either agency's lending. Their loan records are every loan the FHA insured from 1935 to 1940
  in Baltimore, Peoria and Greensboro, North Carolina [[Fishback et al. 2024](#sources);
  [Rose 2022](#sources)]. The FHA's early underwriting manuals, the first in 1935, advised
  against insuring loans in areas that were racially mixed or might become so
  [[Fishback et al. 2024](#sources); [Hillier 2003](#sources)].

Under the dispute lies a question of fact: did private lenders see the HOLC's maps? Amy Hillier
found that the maps were not widely distributed and that none were given to private interests,
though some private lenders and the FHA had access to them. She concluded that lenders were
already avoiding areas they considered high-risk before the maps existed, and in Philadelphia she
found that the grades explained differences in interest rates but not other lending patterns
[[Hillier 2003](#sources)]. Fishback and coauthors write that the HOLC kept its maps
confidential inside the government's housing agencies, while the FHA shared its own maps and
lending practices with lenders and the public [[Fishback et al. 2023](#sources)]. Aaronson and
coauthors write that the degree to which lenders used the actual maps will likely never be known
[[Aaronson et al. 2021](#sources)]. Todd Michney and LaDale Winling have written on how the maps
were made and the professional networks behind them; their articles are listed under Sources for readers to consult and are not
summarized here, because this page could not read them [[Michney 2022](#sources);
[Winling and Michney 2021](#sources)].

Whichever reading holds, Richmond's own record runs before and after the map. The local
histories set out the city's own lines: the 1871 wards, the 1911 and later ordinances, covenants
until 1948, Harland Bartholomew's 1946 master plan for the city, the siting of public housing,
the Turnpike, urban renewal, and the 1970 annexation of 23 square miles of Chesterfield County
[[LVA timeline](#sources); [Gray-Sparks](#sources)].

In 1937 the twelve D forms' mortgage-money field, as reported by the local real estate men who
supplied the forms, reads from "None" in D7 to 60% of appraisal in D1, D2, D4 and D11, with 50%
in D6, D8 and D10, 40% in D5 and 35 to 40% in D11.5; it is blank on D3 and D9. A5's reads "Good"
[[FRASER, Richmond area descriptions](#sources)]. Whether the HOLC or the FHA lent in Richmond's
own D areas after 1937 has not been measured for this page.

## What the drawing adds to a flat map

Readers who know the 1937 maps have seen them flat, as colored shapes on a street map. Drawn on
the ground, three things show that a flat map leaves out.

- **The ground under the grades.** The grades are draped on 2010s lidar and measured on it: the D
  land lies lowest, and the A land is the steepest.
- **The buildings of 1937 at their true size.** Lot sizes read directly, as in the Windsor Farms
  and Jackson Ward figures. Density in 1937 reads only where the Sanborn maps were traced: there
  Jackson Ward is drawn building by building as it stood, and only {{jw_pct}}% of its 1952
  outlines stand today.
- **The highways measured against the grades.** The interstates and the Downtown Expressway are
  drawn over the grades and measured inside them, area by area, against what each grade's
  location alone would predict.

The outlines themselves were redrawn from the National Archives scan and checked against
*Mapping Inequality*'s. The method and its gaps are set out next.

## How we know: the redrawn map, the buildings and the measures

### The redrawn map and the forms

- **The grades.** The outlines were redrawn in code, with Claude, from the National Archives'
  public-domain scan of the 1937 map (Record Group 195, via FRASER, the Federal Reserve Bank of
  St. Louis's digital library): each tint on the scan was traced, each area named from its
  printed label, and the whole placed on today's city by 33 street corners that stood in 1937,
  matched to OpenStreetMap. The fit is a single stretch-and-turn (affine) fit with a typical
  error of about 12 m over the 33 corners (root-mean-square): 3 to 5 m in the middle of the
  map, up to about 24 m at its edges [[FRASER, Richmond](#sources); [The redraw's notes](#sources)].
- **Checked against Mapping Inequality.** Compared with *Mapping Inequality*'s digitization, the
  two sets agree closely: grade by grade their land overlaps by 92 to 94%, D is {{D_pct}}% of
  graded land in this set and 30% in theirs, and the highway share in D is the same to within a
  point [[Mapping Inequality](#sources)]. Shifting every outline up to 24 m in any direction moves
  D's share of the highway between {{shift24_lo}}% and {{shift24_hi}}% (between
  {{shift12_lo}}% and {{shift12_hi}}% for 12 m); {{edge_D_km}} km of D's highway lies within 25 m
  of a D edge.
- **Pieces the map leaves unnumbered.** The map numbers 48 areas and prints no number on some
  small tinted pieces. Four of them (a B block at Rodney, Wythe and Bethlehem Roads; two D
  blocks, on Canal and Byrd Streets and by Monroe Park; and a thin D strip along C10) keep their
  grade and belong to no numbered area here, so they count in the totals by grade and in no
  area's own figures. The redraw gave the other unnumbered pieces to the nearest area of their
  grade: a D strip along Meadowbridge Road to D4, the D pieces around D10's circle to D10, a D
  piece of about 24 ha east of D6 to D5, and a small C patch to C10. One D speck of about 0.5 ha,
  2.2 km from any D label, is left out of every total. A5 and A3 share one green field whose
  printed dividing line has gaps; the line between them here is the redraw's estimate and is
  approximate. The story can be rebuilt from either set of outlines.
- **The names of the areas.** The 1937 form has no name field. Where a form carries a typed
  heading with a place name, that heading is the area's historic name, given exactly as the scan
  reads it: A5 Windsor Farms, D6 Church Hill, D7 Fulton, D9 Riverview and D11 South Richmond. The
  names in parentheses are today's, chosen to orient a reader and checked against the City of
  Richmond's civic-group maps; two of D1's are historic (Apostle Town is the neighborhood cleared
  for Gilpin Court, and VCU's campus covers most of Navy Hill), and about {{carver_D1}}% of D1 lies
  in today's Carver. D2's form has no heading, but the D4 form reads "Same as Oak Park, D2." (scan
  p. 40, field 14), and the state's historical marker names Oak Park as one of the two
  developments that became Washington Park [[DHR, Washington Park](#sources)]. D8 is named for
  Randolph alone: about a quarter of D8 lies in the Oregon Hill Historic District, which the
  Valentine describes as white by the mid-1900s, and the D8 form describes a population 95%
  Black [[The Valentine, Oregon Hill](#sources)].
- **The forms' words.** Every form value and quotation this page uses was read on the National
  Archives scan (FRASER, `ad_RichmondVA.pdf`, one page per area in grade order) in November 2026,
  and quotations keep the scan's capitals and spelling. To find our way we used the transcription
  on *Mapping Inequality*, the work of students and volunteers with the University of Richmond's
  Digital Scholarship Lab and its partners, which made these forms searchable. Where the scan and
  the transcription differ, this page follows the scan. The differences that change something on
  this page, each confirmed by two separate readings of the scan, are D4's remark, which names D2
  as Oak Park; D2's family incomes ($350 to $750); D11.5's share of owner-occupied homes (35%);
  and D10's houses, "25 years and older".

### The measures

- **Elevation and slope by grade** are read from the same 5 m lidar grid at every node inside
  each grade's outlines; the grid is today's ground, so the highways' cuts are in it.
- **Areas and lengths** are measured in code on a local plane. Each grade's land is its outlines
  merged. Highway length is OpenStreetMap's highway category (`motorway`), each carriageway
  counted, ramps left out; with ramps, D holds {{hlD}}%. "The citywide view" is the frame of the
  citywide pictures; "the cached data" is the whole OpenStreetMap extract the pictures are drawn
  from, which reaches past the frame. Every percentage is rounded once, from the unrounded
  measure.
- **The fair comparison for the highways.** The center is the Virginia State Capitol, as
  OpenStreetMap outlines it. Distances are read on a 50 m grid. The location-only expectation
  shares the highway inside graded land in each {{ring_km}} km ring about the Capitol among the
  grades by their land in that ring. "Ungraded land" is the citywide view less all graded land.
- **Public housing addresses.** Each court's addresses in its HUD record were matched to
  OpenStreetMap's address points and placed on the 1937 grades.
- **The frame** holds all of A and B, {{fD}}% of D and {{fC}}% of C. C14 lies wholly outside it
  and {{cut_C13}}% of C13; smaller parts of C6 ({{cut_C6}}%), D11.5 ({{cut_D11_5}}%) and C12
  ({{cut_C12}}%) do too. Every area is measured whole, inside the frame or not.
- **The ground** is 2010s lidar, heights drawn three times true. In Jackson Ward the Turnpike's
  cut is filled for 1937 by the estimate the [Jackson Ward story](jackson-ward.md) made.
- **The grade colors** are the HOLC's green, blue, yellow and red, softened; every picture carries
  a key with the four grades as Richmond's legend names them and the HOLC's names in quotation
  marks, so the colors never have to be read alone.
- **The code** is in the isoscape repository: `tools/holc/` redraws the outlines, and
  `tools/stories/redlining/` holds the measures, charts and pictures, with every computed number
  in one file.

## Glossary of terms used on this page

- **HOLC**: the Home Owners' Loan Corporation, a federal agency created in 1933 to refinance the
  mortgages of home owners in distress.
- **FHA**: the Federal Housing Administration, established in 1934, the federal agency that
  insured mortgages made by private lenders.
- **Residential security map**: the title on Richmond's HOLC map, "Residential Security Map",
  and the Library of Virginia's name for the HOLC's color-coded maps, made from 1935 to 1940.
- **Grade**: the HOLC's rating of an area, A to D: First to Fourth Grade on Richmond's map, and
  "Best", "Still Desirable", "Definitely Declining" and "Hazardous" handwritten on the legend of
  the HOLC's map of Atlanta.
- **Area description**: the printed form the surveyors filled in for each graded area.
- **Redlining**: from the red of the D grade; the word for lenders' refusing credit by
  neighborhood.
- **Covenant**: a clause in a deed restricting who may buy or live in a house.
- **Racial Integrity Act**: the Virginia law on race and marriage on which Richmond based a
  housing ordinance, which barred residents from neighborhoods where they could not marry a member
  of the majority population.
- **Urban renewal**: federally funded clearance and rebuilding of areas a city declared
  blighted.
- **Sanborn map**: a fire-insurance map drawn building by building, used here for Jackson Ward.
- **Parcel**: a lot in the city assessor's records, which gives a year built.
- **Lidar**: elevation measured from the air by laser; here, 5 m elevation grids from the US
  Geological Survey.
- **Vertical exaggeration**: heights drawn three times true, so the hills read.
- **OpenStreetMap**: the volunteer-made map of the world, the source of today's buildings,
  streets and highways here.
- **Limited-access highway**: a road entered only by ramps; OpenStreetMap's `motorway`.
- **Carriageway**: one direction of a divided highway; each is counted.
- **FRASER**: the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis's digital library, which holds the National
  Archives scans of the HOLC's maps and forms.
- **ODbL**: the Open Database License, under which OpenStreetMap's data is published.
