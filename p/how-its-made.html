How it's made

isoscape draws a real place as an isometric dot-and-line picture: inked edges, a dot on every visible corner, flat fills. Nothing in it is drawn by hand. Every line comes from two open datasets, OpenStreetMap and the USGS 3D Elevation Program, and the code decides where it goes.

This page follows the pipeline from the download to the finished SVG. It also says, for every part of the picture, whether it was measured, inferred from a measurement, or guessed. They look the same on the page, and they are not.

Each build in the explorer also has its own How this view was made tab under the picture. It gives that view's frame, sources and counts, and links back to the sections here.

Inputs

OpenStreetMap. fetch asks the Overpass API for everything the renderer can draw: buildings and building parts, water and waterways, roads, railways, parks, woods and other land cover, islands, coastline and car parks. Large areas are fetched in tiles of at most 1.6 km a side, because Overpass refuses or times out on big requests. Four public mirrors are tried in turn. Each tile is cached as it arrives, so an interrupted fetch picks up where it stopped. The merged answer is saved as data/<region>/osm.json.

Elevation. In the US, the ground comes from the USGS 3D Elevation Program (3DEP), the default source. Its seamless bare-earth model is served by the National Map's exportImage service, which returns the best data available for each place: 1 m lidar where it has been flown, coarser models elsewhere. Bare earth means buildings and trees have been removed. isoscape requests uncompressed 32-bit float GeoTIFFs in tiles of at most 1,500 pixels a side, at 1 m per pixel unless the region sets demResolution. The tiles are stitched together and stored as data/<region>/dem.bin.gz: gzipped 16-bit integers in centimeters, so heights are kept to the nearest centimeter.

The service keeps its pixels square in degrees. A tile sized in meters is not square in degrees away from the equator, so the service widens it north and south about its center and fills the same number of pixels: the image covers more ground than was asked for. Each tile is read on the box it really covers and resampled onto the box asked for. Until November 2026 this was missed, and the ground in every 3DEP map sat squeezed north–south by the cosine of the latitude (0.79 in Richmond, 0.73 at Crater Lake): up to a few hundred meters off at a tile's edge, with a step where two tiles met. Water outlines then lay on banks and cliffs, which read as rock and as false falls. Files fetched before the fix are put right as they load.

Outside the US, a region names another source in terrain.source; isoscape sources lists them with their credits and licenses, and Landmarks around the world shows one map for each. ign (France, 1 m) is cut to order like 3DEP, by IGN's WMS server, as raw 32-bit heights. The rest are published in fixed pieces that isoscape downloads and resamples onto its own grid. swisstopo (Switzerland, 2 m) comes as one-kilometer squares in the Swiss grid, found through swisstopo's STAC catalog and converted with swisstopo's published formulas, good to about a meter. gsi (Japan) comes as web-map tiles with heights packed into their colors: 5 m lidar first, then 5 m photogrammetry, then the 10 m national model. copernicus (worldwide, 30 m) comes as one-degree squares of cloud-optimized GeoTIFF, read with Deflate and the floating-point predictor. terrain-tiles (worldwide) is a mosaic of open models in web-map tiles. Copernicus is a surface model, so forest and roofs are in its heights. terrain.fillFrom names a second source for whatever the first leaves empty, which is how the Matterhorn's Italian face is filled from Copernicus. The default demResolution is 1 m, or the source's own spacing where that is coarser (30 m for Copernicus and Terrain Tiles). The cache records which source it came from.

3DEP sometimes fills a gap in its coverage with a large negative number. One patch near Shockoe Bottom reads −88 m. Any sample below −5 m is treated as missing, along with a three-pixel rim around it, and filled from its neighbors.

The download covers the frame's circumscribed circle plus a 60 m margin. The camera can turn after the data arrives (see Camera), and the data still covers the frame. Builds never touch the network, so a build can be repeated exactly.

Building footprints, beyond OSM. OSM is thorough in cities and thin in the country. Around Gwynn's Island it maps 417 buildings, while Microsoft's Global ML Building Footprints find over 6,000. A region can list "microsoft" in sources.buildings. fetch then downloads the dataset's parts for the region's zoom-9 quadkeys, keeps the footprints whose centers fall in the region, and stores them in data/<region>/footprints.json. The model estimates a height for many of them, and that height is kept.

Land cover. Rural OSM rarely maps woods and fields. A region with sources.landcover: "nlcd" gets the National Land Cover Database (NLCD 2021) through the MRLC's map service. That's a 30 m classification of the whole country: forest, wetland, pasture, crops, scrub and so on. The map image comes back with each class in its legend color, and each color is matched back to its class. The result is stored in data/<region>/landcover.bin.gz.

Code: src/osm.ts, src/dem.ts, src/dem-sources.ts, src/rasters.ts, src/footprints.ts, src/landcover.ts.

Region

A place is one JSON file in regions/. It sets the center (longitude, latitude), the frame's width and depth in meters, the camera's bearing and whether it may snap, the scale in pixels per meter, the tilt, the terrain settings, rules for bridge decks, and the palette. The README's "Region config" section lists every key.

Positions are converted to local meters east and north of the center. This simple equirectangular approximation is accurate to a fraction of a meter across a city-sized frame.

Code: src/region.ts.

Camera

The camera is orthographic: no perspective, so a building is the same size near and far, and lengths across the picture can be measured with a ruler. It looks down at a fixed tilt. The region sets squash, the sine of that tilt:

  • Ground across the picture is drawn at full scale (scale pixels per meter).
  • Ground running into the picture is drawn at squash of its length. At 0.5 the tilt is 30°, the classic 2:1 view of isometric games.
  • Heights are drawn at the cosine of the tilt (0.87 at 30°), times any exaggeration.

The frame is a rectangle on the ground, square to the camera, so it lands on the screen as a plain rectangle. That suits a panorama.

Snap. Seen from due north, most street grids sit at an awkward angle. With snap on, isoscape measures the dominant direction of every building edge in the frame (weighted by length, folded so that edges at 0°, 90°, 180° and 270° all count for the same grid). The camera then turns by up to 45° to put that grid on the picture's diagonals. Downtown Richmond snaps to a bearing of −8.7°. Set "snap": false to keep the bearing you gave.

Code: src/view.ts, chooseBearing in src/scene.ts.

Ground

Each OSM area is classified by its tags as water, island, park or grass, wood, marsh, sand, bare rock, parking or cemetery, and drawn as a flat fill in that order. Cover that lies mostly over water (the trees on a river island) is drawn over the water; the rest is drawn under it, so a park mapped around a lake, as Central Park is around the Lake, does not paint the lake over as lawn. Rivers are drawn from their mapped area, never from their center line. Canals, streams and ditches are drawn as ribbons of a typical width.

Roads and rail become ribbons. A road's width comes from its width tag if it has one, otherwise from its class: 14 m for a motorway, 7 m for a residential street, 1.8 m for a footway. Tunnels are left out. Railways are 3.2 m ribbons with dashed ties. Weirs and dams are drawn as white crests.

Land cover from NLCD, where a region uses it, goes down first, between the bare ground and OSM's own areas. Each 30 m class is resampled onto the terrain grid and grouped as woods, evergreen woods, swamp, marsh, field, crop, scrub or barren. The groups are then blurred, a Gaussian of 12 m (0.4 of a 30 m cell), and a group covers the ground wherever it outweighs every other group and bare ground; the edge is traced with marching squares between the grid nodes. A diagonal edge of 30 m steps comes out within about a meter and a half of a straight line, so the source's blocks don't show at print size, and a single 30 m cell still keeps a small rounded patch of its own. Two patches that meet share one edge, with no gap or overlap between them. Open water and unknown cells carry no weight, so the cover runs to the shore, where the shoreline cuts it. OSM's parks, woods and marshes are drawn on top of it, so mapped detail wins.

Code: src/classify.ts, buildScene in src/scene.ts, cover in paintStrip in src/render.ts.

Coastline

OSM does not map the sea, or tidal water like the Chesapeake Bay, as water polygons. It maps the coastline as lines with the land always on their left. isoscape joins those lines into chains and cuts them where they cross the frame. It closes each piece of land by walking the frame's edge, and the water is the frame minus the land. The result can be wrong if the coastline near the frame is broken or mis-tagged.

Code: src/coast.ts.

Terrain

With terrain turned on, the elevation is resampled onto a regular grid in the camera's coordinates, every 5 m by default (cell). A light blur removes the curbs, rubble and noise that 1 m lidar records and a 5 m grid would turn into speckle.

The scene's height at any point is its elevation above the lowest point in the frame (the datum), times terrain.exaggeration. The sample regions draw hills 2.5–4× taller than life, so the landform reads from a distance. Buildings and bridges keep their true heights on top of the exaggerated ground, so a 2.5× hill does not stretch the towers standing on it.

Contours are computed from the true elevation; the hillshade from the drawn, exaggerated surface, so it matches the shape you see:

  • Hillshade. The ground is lit from the left and a little toward the camera. The light is worked out at every grid node, from the surface smoothed once over its neighbors (a 3×3 blur), so it changes smoothly from node to node. The shading is then drawn as nested bands, two to each of 12 steps lighter or darker (24 each way), whose edges are traced with marching squares between the nodes. Each band adds a thin wash on top of the one it sits in, so the ground shows smooth, soft shading rather than 5 m squares, and no two shades meet edge to edge. The shading stops exactly at the traced shoreline: the water is cut out of it, and its bands bend where they cross the shoreline, as the ground does. It is not drawn on the river. Steep ground is held back: past 5 steps either way the shade grows only logarithmically (5 + 2 · ln(1 + (s − 5)/2) steps for a slope worth s), so a bluff, cut or embankment turned from the light darkens smoothly instead of piling up as a dark smear. A face that would be 17 steps dark shows about 9, one at 40 about 11. Gentle ground stays under the knee and is drawn exactly as it was; lit slopes never pass about 6 steps, however steep, so they change by less than a sixth of a step.
  • Contours. Marching squares every 2 m by default (contour), with every fifth line heavier (majorEvery). There are no contours over water: they are cut at the shoreline too.

Code: src/terrain.ts, paintStrip in src/render.ts.

Rivers

Lidar over a river is not the river. 3DEP's bare-earth surfaces are mostly hydro-flattened, level across the water and stepping down along it. But OSM's water outline never matches them to the meter, and rocks, ledges and overlapped banks stand above the water. Draped naively, the river would climb its own banks.

So the river's surface is recovered. At each water point on the grid, it is taken as a low value (the 15th percentile) of the elevations of all water points within 30 m. It is then smoothed along the water. The river ends up level across its width and falling along its course, steeply only where it really drops.

  • Water points standing more than 1 m above that surface, at least two grid cells (10 m at the default 5 m grid) from shore, are drawn as exposed rock. These are the boulder fields of the falls. Each boulder sits up to a third of a cell off its grid point, chosen by a hash of the point, so a field of them does not read as a honeycomb in rows. Where a hydro-flattened river steps down, the water just above the step stands clear of a surface read partly below it; so a rock must also stand more than 1 m above a low value (the 15th percentile) of the water within 30 m that is level with it or further up the channel. Otherwise every step would be drawn as a line of boulders.

  • Only a river gets rocks and rapids. Water mapped as a river (water=river, rapids or stream, or waterway=riverbank), or plain natural=water with a river or stream line running through it, is a river. A lake, pond, reservoir, basin, treatment works, canal, dock, pool or fountain, and the sea, are still water: they get neither.

  • Nothing is drawn as rock on ground painted over the river (a bed of bare rock, a wooded island the water outline left in), or within 20 m of a mapped weir or dam: the lidar sees the dam standing clear of the water, but it is drawn as a crest, not as boulders.

  • Near the shore, mapped "water" standing more than 1 m above the surface is treated as bank. It is given back to the land at its own height.

  • Where the elevation source has no heights (3DEP leaves much of San Francisco Bay empty), the gap is filled in from the shore around it. That fill is a guess, not a surface, so no rock is read within 30 m of it, and no fall is read on it or from it.

Both are inferences: a gravel bar mapped as water or a misplaced outline can produce false rock. Flat regions skip all of this and draw OSM's outlines as they are.

Code: settleWater in src/terrain.ts.

Rapids

A rapid is where the recovered water surface falls quickly along the river. 3DEP's water is hydro-flattened: level from bank to bank, stepping down along the river by a meter or less at a time, with long level reaches between the steps. Measured across a few cells, the fall is all at the step lines and nil between them, which is not how a river runs.

So the fall is measured along the channel, over a reach. First, every point of a river gets its distance up the channel: the shortest path through the river's own water from where it rises out of its lowest pool (the water within 5 cm of its lowest level; in Richmond, the tidal James below the Mayo Bridge). Then, at each water point at least two grid cells from shore, the surface heights of the river around it, within 200 m and from 50 m below it to 200 m above it along the channel, are fitted against that distance. The slope of the fit is the fall. This spreads each step's drop over the 200 m below it, where the water runs white, and reads the pool above a dam as calm to within 50 m of its crest. Where the fall exceeds terrain.rapids (0.004 by default, 4 m per km), the point reads as a rapid. For comparison, the James falls about 32 m (105 feet) through Richmond, a little over 3 m per km on average, and its level pools fall about 1 m per km.

Each rapid point gets short white strokes across the current, as thick as the fall: a stroke in about one point in seven just over the threshold, rising to one in most points and two in some at four times it (16 m per km), with a faint wash of froth that fades in over the steepest. So a reach reads as broken water and not as even hatching along the steps of the lidar's surface, each stroke sits anywhere in its 5 m cell, turns up to 20° off square to the current and runs 2 to 7 m long; a smooth field of clumps about 20 m across scales how many points get one (0.4 to 1.6 times, 1 on average), and each point's froth is a soft, uneven blob off its center rather than a square. Only points over the threshold ever get a mark. The strokes are placed by a hash of the point's position, not at random, so the same data always draws the same marks. No rapid is drawn on ground painted over the river. Weirs mapped in OSM are drawn separately.

Rapids are inferred, not observed. They show where the lidar surface falls, which is usually where the water is white, but river level on the day of the survey, where the hydro-flattening put its steps, or a dam can all mislead it. Until November 2026 the fall was measured across 40 m against a threshold of 3.5 m per 100 m; once the elevation was put where it belongs, nothing on the James fell that steeply, and the rapids drawn before had mostly come from water outlines lying on banks. The build's tab reports how many points were read as rapids.

Code: channelFall and riverFall in src/terrain.ts, paintStrip in src/render.ts.

Shoreline

With terrain, the water's edge follows OSM's outline, traced between the grid's nodes: each node holds its distance to the nearest edge of the mapped water and islands (positive over water, out to two cells either way), and the shoreline is drawn where that crosses zero, which is the outline itself, a straight edge exactly. A grid that only knows wet and dry nodes can put the shore only halfway between them, and a shore running slantwise across the grid came out as a staircase, a run of nodes long and a cell high; on a steep, exaggerated bank each step stood up as a tooth.

The lidar still corrects the outline where it has to. Mapped water that the lidar finds more than 1.5 m above the river (a bluff the outline overlaps, a creek in its ravine), or lower than that but more than two cells from the outline (a bar or a rock in midstream), stays land, and there the shore is traced as before, through the wet and dry nodes smoothed over 3 × 3. Mapped water lower than that and near the outline is drawn as water.

The ground meets the water on the shoreline. On the water's side of it the ground lies on the river's surface; on the land's side it rises from the surface to its own height one cell inland, in proportion to the distance. A shore cell is drawn as two pieces that meet on the line, land and water, and anything else draped across the line (cover, parks, roads, the hillshade's bands) gets a point where it crosses, so it bends there with the ground and does not stand clear of it, over the water behind.

Narrow water follows its outline too: a canal a cell or two wide keeps its mapped banks instead of ending in shards cut along the grid. Water so narrow that no grid node falls in it (a stream mapped as an area under a cell wide) is still not drawn.

Code: shoreField and Terrain.z in src/terrain.ts, paintStrip in src/render.ts.

Buildings

Every OSM building that lies wholly inside the frame and covers at least minBuildingArea (12 m² by default) becomes a prism. Walls are drawn on the sides facing the camera, and the roof goes on top. Courtyards work. Wall shading follows which way each wall faces, with the light from the left. Roof color follows the building's type: homes, commerce, industry or civic. Where a building is mapped as Simple 3D Buildings parts (building:part), the parts are drawn instead of the outline if the parts that reach the ground cover at least half of it. Many outlines carry parts only for a crown, a tower or rooftop works; such an outline is drawn whole, and its parts only above its roof. Parts that are only roofs (building:part=roof) never replace the outline: it stays as their podium. A building is drawn whole or not at all, so parts whose outline the frame cuts are left out too. Nothing stands on air: a part raised off the ground (min_height) that nothing beside it holds up, and that is not mapped as a bridge, reaches down to the roof under it or to the ground (a crown whose shaft is missing from the data, or too small to draw). A bridge tower's parts (tower:type=bridge) are often mapped from the top of the pier up, with the pier left out, so the lowest of them (no tower part within 40 m starts lower) reach down to the ground or water; struts between the legs keep their height. Open canopies (building=roof) are left out.

Two shapes are not prisms. A lattice tower (tower:construction=lattice, such as the Eiffel Tower) is drawn whole as a spire on its outline, in 16 stacked tapering pieces, in a darker iron tone. The outline is first cleared of corners within 3% of √area of their neighbors' line (about 3 m on the Eiffel Tower), so the small jogs where a leg meets an arch don't run up the tower as slivers, and dots go only at the corners of its foot that face the camera. Its width follows the Eiffel Tower's curve: e^(−3.3·t) of the base at height fraction t, so 0.56 at the first platform and 0.32 at the second, running out to a mast over the top sixth. The parts mapped inside it are dropped. A curved roof shell with no height (a roof part shaped round, spherical or circular, such as the Sydney Opera House's sails) is drawn as a sail rising to a point, 1.6 × √area meters above its podium. That height is an approximation, not a survey.

Footprints from Microsoft's model fill in where OSM has none. Wherever an OSM building and a footprint overlap, OSM wins and the footprint is dropped. A footprint is dropped if its center lies in an OSM building, or if it holds an OSM building's center. A footprint whose center is on water is dropped too: there the model has found a boat, a pier head or a boathouse it cannot tell from a house. Buildings mapped in OSM on water, such as boathouses, stay.

Heights are taken from the first of these that the building has:

  1. a height tag, from a survey or a mapper's estimate (a whole building tagged under 2 m, or with a height that can't be read, is taken as having none: Los Angeles County's import gives hundreds of hillside houses 0.4 m). In maps that turn on checkLevels (downtown Richmond), an apartment building, dormitory, hotel, house, terrace or office tagged more than 5.5 m a floor is taken as mistagged and drawn by its floors instead: there a six-story apartment building is tagged 80 m and a twelve-story one 96 m;
  2. an estimate from Microsoft's model, for its own footprints, where it made one;
  3. building:levels, at 3.4 m a floor, plus half a floor for each roof level;
  4. a guess from the building's type and size: 3.2 m for a shed or anything under 40 m², 8 m for a house, 10 m for a terrace, 16 m for apartments or a hotel, 13 m for an office (24 m over 1,500 m²), 11 m for industry, 16 m for a church, and 8–14 m by footprint for anything else.

Each build counts how many heights came from each source. A view with many guesses has the right footprints and plausible but invented skylines. Joining real heights from lidar (the first-return surface minus bare earth) is the top item on the roadmap.

On a slope, a building stands on the lowest ground under it, so its walls never float. The ground is drawn steeper than life but buildings keep their true heights, so the roof is set by the true slope, not the drawn one: it sits at the building's height above the lowest ground plus half the true fall across the footprint (the drawn fall divided by the exaggeration). On the downhill side the building shows what it would on the real hill, and the rest of the drawn relief goes into the hill on its uphill side, so a two-story house on a steep street stays a two-story house. On flat ground nothing changes. The parts of one building all count their heights from the level of its outline, and buildings traced from fire-insurance maps and lost landmarks are seated the same way.

A building that spans water, one OpenStreetMap gives a layer of 1 or more and that has mapped water under its center (a mill or a house over a canal), stands on the banks: in maps that turn this on (seatOnBanks; downtown Richmond), its height counts from the ground at its corners on land, not from the bed of the water under it. Its walls still reach down to the water. In downtown Richmond this moves one building, over the Haxall Canal, from a roof below the bank in front of it to a roof 4 m above it.

Code: buildingHeights in src/classify.ts, extrude and spire in src/scene.ts.

Bridges

OSM splits one bridge into many ways, at every change of lanes, name or junction. Ramping each piece down to the ground would turn a viaduct into a roller coaster. So bridge ways that share an end, a layer and a kind (road or rail) are joined into chains first. At a fork a chain carries on along the straightest way, and never through a turn sharper than 60 degrees, so two tracks that meet are not joined into a V. A chain only ramps down at an end that touches no other bridge. Bridges that touch form a network, anchored to the ground at those ends, and no deck stands higher than a 70 m ramp from the nearest anchor allows, measured along the network: a viaduct that forks into short ways that each come down a few meters on ramps down to the fork, not standing at full height over a fan of steep stubs. An end that joins another bridge (a ramp onto a viaduct of another layer, a link at a fork) meets the higher of the decks there and eases to its own height over 70 m. Then the decks are matched at every node they share: a deck that runs on through the node sets the height there, and where only ends meet, the highest end does. The others run straight to that height, so no deck stops short in mid-air above or below the one it joins. Where a deck comes down to the ground, its side stops at the ground instead of hanging below it.

A deck runs straight between its ends. It never dips below the straight line between the heights of its two ends, and rises above that line only where it needs its clearance: an overpass whose ends are both at street level spans a cut level, whatever depth the cut has, and ends at different heights give a straight grade. A grounded end sits on its abutment. In a smoothed ground model an overpass mapped rim to rim ends on the sloping sides of the cut, so the ground is read for 12 m past the end: where it climbs within the first 6 m and then levels off (the outer 6 m climbs less than three quarters as much), the end sits at that level, with an abutment wall down to the ground when the end faces the camera. Where the ground goes on climbing (a ramp landing on a hillside), the end sits on the ground.

Ground the model kept under a deck is carved out. A bare-earth model is meant to have bridges taken out, but a short overpass often stays in it as a ridge of ground across the cut it spans, and the road under it then climbs over a hump. Under every deck, a grid node that stands more than half a meter above the ground 8 m past the edges of the decks on both sides takes the straight line across between those two. The carving fades in over the 10 m nearest a grounded end, so the street running onto a bridge keeps its level, and it never raises ground: a deck along a hillside or a bluff's crest changes nothing.

Parallel ways of one street are one deck. OSM maps a street's carriageways and its sidewalks or cycle tracks as ways of their own, side by side, and each would get a profile of its own: a sidewalk sitting lower beside its street, a divided street as a cluster of tilted slabs. A footway, path or cycleway bridge that lies within 15 m of a street's bridge its whole length, running the same way along it, takes the street's heights wherever it lies beside it (a street before a ramp beside it); so does a carriageway beside a longer one of the same name, kind and layer. A bridge way shorter than 10 m that shares a node with a longer deck is drawn level, at that deck's height at the node.

Skyways are left out. A footway, path or cycleway bridge no longer than 80 m with an end in or within 3 m of a building is a skyway or a bridge into an upper floor, and OSM rarely says which floor. Drawn at a ramp's height, it lies across the walls and roofs of the buildings it joins, so it is not drawn (sidewalks beside a street bridge are kept). It is decided from the buildings' outlines before the ground is carved, so the ground under a skyway is left as it is.

Deck height is a rule of thumb. OSM rarely records it. A region can name a height for a bridge, for example { "match": "Manchester", "deck": 24 }. Otherwise the deck sits bridgeDeck meters (7 by default) above the ground for each OSM layer. The deck is a taut line over whatever it crosses: never closer to the ground than its clearance, rising in straight runs, and easing down over 70 m at a free end. The ramp keeps that length on a short bridge, so a bridge shorter than two ramps never reaches full height: a 30 m footbridge over a canal rises well under a meter above its banks, where a 7 m tent would read as a stub floating in the air. Piers drop to the real ground or water every 38 m where the deck is more than 3 m up, or to the roof of a building under the deck, never down through it. Each pier is ordered on its own, behind its deck and behind a building nearer the camera. A span that lies inside a building and below its roof (a garage ramp, a walkway's landing) is left out: the building hides it.

Each bridge is cut into spans of at most 22 m, so a long bridge can pass in front of one building and behind another. Bridges are cut at the edges of the frame.

A suspension bridge (bridge:structure=suspension) whose towers are mapped as parts (tower:type=bridge) gets its two main cables, drawn as plain lines. The parts standing within 25 m of the bridge's line make its towers, and the parts either side of the widest gap across the line are a tower's two legs. Each cable runs from an anchorage on the deck at one end of the bridge, over the tops of the legs, to the other end: a parabola between towers that sags to 2 m over the deck at mid-span, and a slighter one down to each anchorage. Suspenders, tower detail and arches are not drawn.

Code: carveUnderBridges, isSkyway, chainBridges, joinDecks, groupDecks, deckProfile, abutmentLevel and bridgePieces in src/scene.ts.

Paint order

The drawing has no depth buffer. Things are painted back to front, and the order is the hard part. Sorting by a single depth per object fails as soon as a long warehouse sits beside a small house. So the order is decided pairwise, only between objects whose boxes overlap on screen:

  • If their footprints can be separated by a line, the one on the camera's side of that line is in front.
  • If the footprints overlap (stacked building parts), the one entirely above is in front.
  • A pier and a deck whose footprints overlap: the deck is in front (a deck's height range starts at its underside, so a deck over another deck is in front of it too).
  • Two bridge spans that run side by side (within about 14 degrees of parallel, the two carriageways of a viaduct or a pair of tracks): the one whose line is nearer the camera is in front, or the upper one where one runs right over the other. By centers alone, two overlapping decks alternate span by span and read as a ladder.
  • Otherwise the nearer center wins.

A topological sort turns these pairwise results into one order. The rare cycle, from concave footprints that interlock, is broken by depth.

Code: src/order.ts.

Drawing

The ground is painted in strips, two grid rows deep (10 m at the default cell size), from the back of the frame to the front. A landscape hides parts of itself: a bank that falls away from the camera hides the water behind it. Each strip drapes everything that lies on it over the terrain: bare ground, parks, water, roads, rail, hillshade, contours, rocks and rapids. Each building or bridge is painted straight after the strip that holds its front edge, so a hill in front of it covers it and the ground behind it does not.

The next strip's parks, roads and cover lap 0.6 m back over a strip's near edge, so a strip's hillshade and contours are shifted the same way: each runs from just past its near edge (0.6 m plus the width of the edge strokes, about 1.1 m at the sample scale) to the same distance past its far edge. The ground in that lap is then shaded once, by the strip that painted it last, and no light streak shows along the strip lines. Where two strips' shading meets, both trace the same points, so there is no hairline between them.

Within a strip, the water is one path: the wholly wet cells and the wet parts of shore cells together, so they meet without a seam. Cover and parks that lie under water (a park drawn around a lake) are cut out where the water is, so nothing of theirs shows through where one strip's water meets the next's; on a lake those seams had shown as faint lines.

Each park, wood or other ground shape is cut to the strip as one polygon, holes and all, and comes out as its separate pieces. Cut one outline at a time, a wood with a clearing or a notch of bare ground in it kept an edge along the strip's line across the clearing, enclosing nothing; since ground shapes are outlined in their own color (0.6 units wide) to close the seams between them, each such edge showed as a green line, and the clearing was ruled with them one strip apart.

The ground reaches a cell past the plate's sides, and the sides are then covered in paper, so each side is one clean edge. (Cut by a clip, a renderer that clips each shape in turn laid the edge once for every shape that reached it, and a side over water showed a dotted line.) A plate with no slab (a cutaway) is cut square by its own rectangle instead, and its ground ends at its sides.

The front of the plate is a slab, a true cross-section of the ground along the near edge, with water where the cut crosses water at least two cells wide, from shoreline to shoreline. A stream narrower than that cuts no stripe down the slab. A region with no slab ("slab": 0, the cutaway) is cut square instead: the ground ends on straight lines, at the lowest point of its far edge on screen and the highest of its near edge, so its edge does not wander with the ground, and standing things are cut along the same near line.

The SVG is compact. Coordinates are rounded to a tenth of a pixel and written relative to the previous point. Draped edges are simplified to a quarter pixel, and the soft edges of hillshade bands to half a pixel: a point is dropped only while the line that replaces it passes within that distance of every point it stands for. (Until November 2026 each point was tested against its two neighbors alone, so a long, gently curving edge could drift several pixels off; across open water, where nothing covers the strips, the gaps showed as white stripes.) Each dot is a zero-length line with round caps. Even so, a big region can produce a file of 25–45 MB. The --png option renders a raster with rsvg-convert. Colors and line weights come from a palette (paper or night).

Code: src/render.ts, src/palette.ts.

Time

A region with a time section is drawn as a series: one scene for each year (epoch) in its history file, built with isoscape series <region>. The downtown Richmond series runs from the 1737 town plan to today. Each scene combines two kinds of evidence, and the explorer says which is which.

Measured: buildings standing today, by the year each was built. A building appears in every scene from its year on. The year comes from:

  1. a curated year, for buildings whose records are known to be wrong or missing (the Capitol, St. John's Church, the Old Stone House, and every tall downtown building: see below), each with a source. A curated year can also end: the Capitol's central block (OSM's middle building part) is drawn alone from 1788, and its full outline, with the wings of 1904–06, from 1906. A curated year can also be unknown: a building whose only date is a parcel's that is known to be wrong, and for which no source gives another, is drawn in today's scene only. A building part (building:part) takes the curated year of the building it belongs to, so a tower's crown is not dated by its parcel apart from the tower;
  2. else OpenStreetMap's start_date tag;
  3. else the city assessor's year built for the parcel under the building's center.

The assessor records one year per parcel, the year of its main building. Up to four buildings on a lot (a row split in OSM, a house and its shed) all take that year. With more buildings, only one clearly larger than the rest takes it. A parcel with eight or more (a campus, Capitol Square) gives its year to none of them, and nor does a parcel holding a curated building. A parking structure (a deck or garage) that only its parcel dates, to a year before 1960, is dated 1960 (parkingFrom), with surface parking: the parcel's year is its lot's earlier building's. Buildings with no year appear only in today's scene.

Tall buildings are checked one by one. In downtown Richmond every building 25 m or taller, or of eight levels or more, drawn in a printed past scene (1865, 1886, and the 1905 to 1990 panels of Richmond, 1737–2026, as well as 1925 and 1950) is dated from a source that names it (the National Register nominations for the Main Street Banking district, SkyscraperPage, Wikipedia and its list of Richmond's tallest buildings, the SAH Archipedia), or is drawn in today's scene only (three parking decks and an annex of eight levels or more, whose only date is their parcel's). The Ross Building at 801 E. Main (eighteen floors, 1965), which its parcel had put in 1925, now first appears in 1970; what stood on its lot before is not known, so the lot is empty in the earlier scenes.

Curated: what is gone or was different. A short list per series, each item with its years and sources: a filled canal basin, a burned district, a buried creek, demolished landmarks drawn from published dimensions in a red-brown tone (a burned shell in ash gray), and the town plan, which limits the earliest scene's streets to the original grid. Positions come from today's street grid. They are not surveyed.

Water that is gone. A filled basin or a canal since drained, drawn on today's ground as it lies, came out as a cliff where the ground stands high and as broken shards where it dips. So curated water, and mapped water whose era ends before today (the canal's downtown end, 1800 to 1890), is drawn as still water at its own level: the median of today's ground within 20 m of it, less 1 m. Every grid node inside it is that water; out to 20 m from its edge the ground eases (a smoothstep in distance) from 1 m above the water back to today's, so a low side becomes an embankment and a high one grades down to a clean bank. The Great Basin is drawn whole until 1880; in 1886 what was left of it (water between 9th and 10th Streets and a narrow basin between 11th and 12th, traced from sheet 11 of the 1886 Sanborn map) and the canal's last stretch into it (sheet 10, and the 1886 key map) are drawn as the map shows them. A canal mapped only as a line is a blue ribbon on the ground.

Bridges that are gone. A curated bridge is drawn as a bridge of today's map would be, from its own line: a road or railroad deck at a stated height over the ground or water under it (the Free Bridge of 1873 to 1972, 8 m, an assumption), on piers, ramping down to the ground at each end, where it lands on a street of its year. Bridges replaced on their old line (Mayo's Bridge before 1913, the Richmond and Petersburg bridge before 1902, the Richmond and Danville's before the Southern's) are drawn on today's line and height in their years, an approximation; none is drawn in 1865, the scene after the fire that burned them. A curated item can be held back from builds made before it existed ("switch"): it is drawn only in a build asked for it by name (isoscape build --with next), and the build records that, so a print already sold is still made exactly as it was. The Free Bridge is drawn only so: the 1925 and 1950 prints, version 4, were drawn without it. Its line is approximate, so in builds made --with next,next2 (the 1925 and 1950 prints' version 5) a traced building with any part within 12 m of it is left out, as one under a rail viaduct is: otherwise its deck ran over the roof of a frame building on the canal bank.

Traced: buildings from fire-insurance maps. The Sanborn Map Company drew every building in a city's business district for insurers, block by block. Each building appears as a footprint colored by material: pink brick, yellow frame, blue stone, gray iron. The Library of Congress has Richmond editions from 1886 to 1925 online. isoscape traces them:

  1. Georeference. Each sheet is placed on the ground by a rotation and shift: at one scale per edition, two or three named street corners read off the sheet are enough. A third corner checks the first two. The block outlines from the city's GIS are drawn over each sheet to confirm the fit (tools/sanborn/rigid.py, ovmosaic.py).
  2. Trace. The colored fills are separated from the paper and the printed lines, read for material, closed over the lettering, and traced as polygons. Near-rectangles are squared up, and outlines and thin strips (river banks, rails) are dropped (extract.py).
  3. Follow through time. A traced footprint mostly covered by a building standing today is taken to be that building, or its replacement on the same footprint, and today's data already draws it. The rest are lost buildings. Each is drawn from the first edition that shows it, though it may be older. It is drawn until it is known to be gone, in this order of preference: a later edition that maps the spot without it; the year the building on its site today went up; the opening of an expressway through it. Failing all of those, it is assumed gone by 1960, when clearing for parking and highways began (lifespans.py).
  4. Clean. A last pass drops slivers, any footprint more than 8 times longer than it is wide and under 12 m wide, or over 100 m long and under 8 m wide on average: on the sheets these are rail viaducts, track-side sheds and street edges, and extruded they would stand as walls. It then smooths each outline to within 1.5 m, so pixel stair-steps do not draw as striped walls. It also drops sprawls (over 2,000 m² and filling under 45% of their convex hull: river shading, not buildings) and merges footprints traced in two editions that overlap by more than 85%, keeping the earlier start and the later end (clean.py: 68 dropped, 170 merged).
  5. Shapes. A second pass (clean.py --shapes) drops the strips the first missed: a bent ribbon (filling under 30% of its smallest enclosing rectangle and over 60 m long), and anything under 4 m across at its widest and at least four times as long; and sawtooth outlines, over 1.35 times the length of their convex hull's while filling under 70% of it, on a strip 80 m long or more (a hatched edge read tooth by tooth) or on a footprint under 1,000 m² (a star of spikes around a shed). It trims spikes with an opening of 1.5 m (shrink, then grow back with square corners), which takes off anything under 3 m wide; a footprint left with under 60% of its area is dropped. Of the 2,604 footprints, 41 strips, 50 sawtooth outlines and 21 spiky footprints were dropped and 247 trimmed; of those standing in 1925, 45 were dropped and 85 trimmed, and in 1950, 49 and 103.

A sheet whose fit is known to be wrong is left out until it is placed again, and the history file says why: the 1905 sheets 5 and 7, read by eye, put 31 buildings on the Capitol Square lawn, so neither is drawn.

A sheet gives the buildings but not the ground they stood on, and its fit is good to a few meters only. So a traced building that could not have stood where it falls is left out whole, never trimmed, and the explorer counts how many and why:

  • On water. Any part of it on the water drawn in its year: past the traced shoreline, inside today's river or canals (a canal mapped only as a line counts at its drawn width), or a curated basin, read every meter over its footprint and along its edges. Most are mills on the islands and races above the Mayo Bridge and warehouses on the Great Basin's edge. In downtown Richmond 27 in 1925 and 24 in 1950.
  • On a viaduct or a track. Any part of it within 12 m of the center line of a rail viaduct standing in its year (most were mapped in 1886 or 1895 and cleared for the viaducts of 1901, the rest are misplaced by their sheet, and drawn, the deck would pass over their roofs or between their walls), or within a meter of the ballast of a track on the ground: 61 in 1925 and 34 in 1950.
  • Buried. Today's ground more than 3 m above its floor anywhere under it (read every 2 m over its footprint and along its walls, not only at its corners: a slope can stand above a long wall between two low corners), within 60 m of a road built after its year (an interstate's cut or fill), or more than 8 m above it anywhere (two stories and more: no building stood cut that deep into a hill): 62 in 1925 and 55 in 1950.

The historic buildings are sketches, not a survey. A machine traced them from scans of fire-insurance maps, so outlines are approximate, heights are guessed from material, and some buildings are missing, misplaced or merged. The 1924 overview ("congested district") sheet is the least precise source. The historic scenes show the city's shape and density, not each building exactly.

Traced buildings keep the maps' colors, so they read apart from today's. The story counts printed on the sheets are not read yet, so every traced building's height is assumed from its material, at 3.6 m a story: brick three stories, frame two, stone three, iron (gray: sheds and warehouses clad in metal) four, "special" (the maps' other hazards) three, and fire-proof construction (olive-brown, in later editions) six. The dated prints say so under their credit line. The explorer counts, for each scene, how many traced buildings stand and how each one's end date was found.

Five editions are traced: 1886; 1895 and 1905 (sheets read by eye); 1924–25, whose first volume opens with a single "congested district" map of the whole business district (fitted with a rubber sheet through sixteen street corners, each snapped to the city's block outlines); and 1950–52, a corrected reprint whose sheets were matched feature by feature to their 1924–25 versions where those were fitted, and read by eye otherwise. Each later edition turns "assumed" end dates into ones read from a map.

Era rules date everything else that OSM maps: the first rule matching a feature's name, ref or tags decides the spans in which it existed. Interstates appear from 1958, the Downtown Expressway from 1976, the river viaducts from 1901, Mayo's Bridge from 1788 (today's of 1913 drawn for every form, and not in 1865), the Seaboard's line from 1900 and station platforms with Main Street Station (1901). The James River and Kanawha Canal's Richmond Dock appears from 1800 until the railroad took the canal over, the line of its Tidewater Connection from 1854, and the Canal Walk's rebuilt channel from 1999. Interchange ramps follow the interstate and parking aisles and driveways follow surface parking (1960, the year by which traced buildings are assumed cleared); both dates are approximate.

Two more rules look at what lies beside a feature. A road bridge stands only over something there in its year: if every road, railway and waterway mapped under a bridge (its spans of one street joined end to end) is not yet built, the street is drawn at grade instead (and so is a rail bridge standing alone, with track on the ground at both ends: the Richmond District's short span over the canal at Rocketts, which in 1925 and 1950 crossed no canal), so in 1950 Broad Street crosses Shockoe Valley on the ground and the streets over the Downtown Expressway have no bridges. And woods and scrub with 40% or more of their outline within 20 m of a road not yet built are that road's verges (the planted ground of an interchange) and are dated with it. (In builds made --with next, the corrections made since the 1925 and 1950 prints' version 4, half the outline within 60 m: the planted banks of the interstate and the expressway that the tighter test missed.) A feature no rule matches is drawn in every year, so the Richmond series ends its rules with catch-alls: the street grid, streams and the river are drawn in every year, and anything else with no dated rule (parks, cemeteries, dams and weirs, piers, basins, paths) is drawn only today, since nothing says when it was made. Each catch-all names what still needs a source. Railways out of use or taken up today (abandoned, disused, razed) are drawn today only, unless a rule dates one from a source (the Richmond and Petersburg bridge over the James, 1838–1970 but for its year burned, with its approach to Byrd Street Station curated as approximate track, since nothing of it is mapped today): their fragments end in open ground. Roundabouts appear from 1990, when the modern roundabout came to the United States, and unnamed service roads of no particular kind (the drives and rings of parking lots) with surface parking, from 1960; both are approximate.

Past years look at their neighbors once more:

  • a minor road bridge (residential, service, unclassified) over a canal, with no start_date, appears only from 1999, the Canal Walk (canalBridgesFrom), unless a rule names it;
  • a road bridge whose spans do not meet a road of the year at each of their ends (a tunnel does not count: it is not drawn) is left out: a piece of deck whose approaches came later;
  • a bed of scrub, wood, grass or heath, or a pond, under 2,000 m² and unnamed is landscaping, dated with the nearest dated building within 40 m (the development around it); with none that near it is natural ground (a thicket on a river island) and is drawn in every year;
  • a rail spur, siding or yard track that ends with no building of the year within 40 m of its end is left out (and the spurs that only led to it): today's spurs serve today's buildings;
  • a rail deck's grounded end sits on the ground there, where its track runs on, not on the level of the bank beyond it (the abutment rule for streets over a cut, below): seated on the bank, the river viaducts' decks ended a few meters above the track they join;
  • a building's walls that face the camera with both corners above its lowest corner stop, level, at the lower of the two, where the ground in front of them starts. Run down to the lowest corner, as in today's scene, the ground painted in front of such a wall (in a hollow, or beside a later cut) covered it along a strip's straight edge.

In every scene after the first, buildings new since the previous scene have gold roofs.

Streets before the houses. A series can say that minor streets (tertiary and smaller, service roads and paths) are drawn only where a building of the year stands within so many meters of them: 75 m in the 1925 streetcar scene, 60 m in the downtown series. A bridge is never dropped by this test: it crosses water or a valley where no house stands, and its era rule alone says when it stood (Mayo's Bridge from 1788); nor is a street that meets a road bridge (the road across Mayo's Island between two spans, a bridge's approach; in builds made --with next, the whole of that street by name, as Hull Street across Manchester). Each street is walked every half that distance and kept if any building (standing today and built by then, traced from a Sanborn sheet and still standing, or a curated landmark) has a corner within one grid cell of a step. A suburb whose streets were laid out but not yet built on shows its main roads only. The downtown series holds its main roads to the same test, because before the first Sanborn map (1886) the only record of how far the town reached is what still stands. A street of a town plan (Mayo's grid of 1737) is kept in every year from the plan on, houses or not, though not the plan's service roads. Today's scene keeps all of today's streets.

Streetcar lines. A curated streetcar item is a line drawn as a bold red ribbon down the street, 9 m wide, with a car every 900 m along it: 14 m long and 3.6 m tall like a 1920s double-truck car, but 5.4 m wide (about twice a real car's width) so its pale roof reads against the line. A series can scale both up so they read across a whole city: the 1925 scene draws them three times life size (27 m lines, 42 m cars). The lines come from tools/streetcars/build.py, which reads each line as a list of street corners (from the 1925 plate of the city's early transit routes and the sources cited per line), finds each corner in OpenStreetMap, and follows the streets between them, preferring the streets the two corners name (any other street costs six times as much per meter). Two streets that meet without sharing a node take their closest points, up to 150 m apart. A run over a viaduct since torn down is drawn straight. Vanished pleasure grounds (a fairground) take a sandy tint of their own, and a racetrack is a closed dirt-colored ribbon. --streetcar-scale overrides the series' scale for one build: the single-stop prints of the streetcar series use 1.5, so the lines read thinner up close.

The fair grounds are traced from sheet 468 of the 1924–25 Sanborn atlas (100 feet to the inch, 0.403 m per pixel of the scan, north up, fixed at Hermitage Road and Sherwood Avenue): the outline follows the Boulevard and Hermitage Road to the tip where they meet, the one-mile track's south turn (a radius of 83 m) is taken off the sheet with its straights parallel to the Boulevard, and the north turn is drawn to make up the mile. Thirty-six of the fair's buildings come from the same sheet. A curated site (the fair grounds while the fair stood, a station's loop of tracks) clears today's streets, lots and buildings from within it.

One building, close up

Broad Street Station in 1943 is modeled from parts (tools/stations/broad_street.py): wings, center pavilion, porch, six columns and two piers, entablature, drum and a dome (a quarter circle in ten steps), each with a height above its seat, and the concourse, a deck at the waiting room's level on piers over the tracks: a part can stand at a given elevation and reach up to another, instead of rising from its seat. A part can rest on another: the columns and piers stand on the porch and the entablature on the columns, so on the sloping forecourt all six columns share one base and one top. The station's own colors live in the palette's parts: limestone, the dome's tile, the iron and roofing of the platform canopies, ramp houses, and the trains. Nine tracks follow the surviving platforms through the station and the USGS quadrangles of 1934 and 1956 around the loop, gathering into four and then two within 110 m of each end. Canopies are cut into 12 m pieces, each on a column, 8 m wide and 5.4 m up; trains are a steam engine (18 m, with a cab), its tender (11 m) and 26 m heavyweight cars, 1 m apart. At 1.5 pixels per meter or closer, track is drawn with its ties and its two rails, 1.44 m apart. Each tie is a rectangle on the ground, 2 m across the track and 0.25 m along it, square to the track where it stands and spaced every 0.67 m along the whole line (the spacing carries on through bends), so it foreshortens with the view like everything else; the strips paint the ties with the ground. The rails are the center line offset 0.72 m each way on the ground, mitered at bends so they have no gap or jog. Farther out, ties are a dashed line along the track. A series can draw track wider than life (railScale, or --rail-scale for one build) and in the ties' darker tone, so a network reads across a city: the wide railroad map uses four times. Curated labels name places on the map; the single-place prints of a set leave them out.

Cutaways

A cutaway opens one modeled building along a vertical plane (src/cutaway.ts, src/cutaway-scene.ts). The region names the plane, a point and a compass bearing, and the side of it that is lifted away; the camera stands on that side. Broad Street Station is cut down its axis, the plane through the middle of the portico, the waiting room and the concourse, running at a bearing of 40.8°, with the east half lifted away and the camera looking from that side at a bearing of 342° (regions/broad-street-station-cutaway.json). Only modeled parts whose ids begin with the region's prefix (bss-) are cut, and one canopy on the removed side that is not cut is lifted away whole.

Every part of the building is first made into convex solids: a prism for a wall, a floor or a bench; for a dome, a solid of revolution in slices, one per side of its outline, with an outer and an inner surface; a stair as one block per tread. Each solid is clipped to the half of space behind the plane, and where the clip opens it a new face closes it on the plane: that face is the section, drawn solid in ink (poché). On the removed side the building is not simply gone: everything up to 1.5 m above its first floor stays, clipped there, so the ground floor reads as a plan, its walls cut low with inked tops, its benches and counters whole. A dashed line marks the footprint of what was lifted away, 1.5 m outside the floors, but only where nothing still stands: within 2.5 m of a low wall it would only repeat the wall, and is left out.

The pieces become ordinary items for the paint order (above). A part's pieces behind the plane are one item and its pieces in front another, and each is split again at the first floor, so it takes its place on both sides of the floor's slab. Within an item, faces turned away from the camera are dropped, the rest are painted far to near, and the sections last. A cutaway stacks many parts over others (slabs over piers, the concourse over trains), so its paint order adds one rule: two items apart on the ground, the farther wholly above the nearer, cannot cover each other and are left unordered; footprints that overlap by up to 3 cm, the rounding of their corners, count as apart. Cars and canopy runs are cut into pieces at most 7 m long, drawn without seams, so each piece takes its own place among the stairs and the deck it passes.

In a cutaway, coaches are solid Pullman-green bodies under a darker arched roof, with a continuous band of windows down each side and, in the 1 m gap ahead of each car that has one coupled ahead of it, the dark diaphragm of its vestibule. A steam engine faces the way its train came in (the engine leads, toward the start of its line): a round boiler with a darker smokebox at the front, a stack and two domes on top, a frame and running board under it, and a boxy cab 4 m long at the back, beside the tender, whose tank is lower than the cab and carries its coal heaped at the front. They are drawn in a grey light enough for their edges to show. Sheds have butterfly roofs falling to a valley over their posts; a post stands only under a roof that is drawn. The ground is flat-toned (the region's terrain.shade is 0, which turns the hillshade off), and the dashed outline of what was lifted away lies level at the first floor.

The flat section is made from the same parts and the same plane: the camera looks level and square at the plane from the removed side, nothing in front of it is kept, and behind it the building is drawn 35 m deep. Trains and sheds are not cut on the skew: where a track with a train standing across the plane, or within 16 m behind it, meets the plane, one car is drawn end on, square and true size on its trucks (a coach's arched end with its door, an engine's round smokebox, or a tender's tank and coal); and where a shed's line meets it, its butterfly profile, a center post and two wings rising outward. A stair down from the floor on the removed side (to the platforms) is drawn as its stepped profile, dashed. Under it the ground is cut along the plane and dug out where the building goes below it; platforms are drawn 0.35 m over the rails (approximate), rails at true gauge where tracks cross the plane, and a red dashed line follows the traveler from the street to the train. The line runs at waist height, 0.95 m over each floor, with a small arrowhead pointing the way on each stretch, and it is broken where it meets a traveler and where it would cross a car end seen square on, so it passes behind the car rather than over its face.

Travelers. A plain standing figure, 1.75 m (5 ft 9 in) tall, in the route's red, gives the drawings their scale: head, body, legs in a short stride and a small suitcase (src/traveler.ts). Its height is a convention, not a claim from a source. In the flat section four stand on the route, feet on their floors: on Broad Street, in the White Waiting Room, on the concourse and at the foot of the stair beside the car end. In the cutaway three stand upright, facing across the picture, on the portico, among the benches of the waiting room and on a platform by the train; each is an item in the paint order like any other, with a 0.5 m footprint, so what stands in front of it covers it. For Broad Street the section runs from 45 m on the street side of the plane's point to 104 m on the track side, at 10 pixels to the meter, and its earth is cut 5 m below the lowest ground along it. Code: src/section.ts.

What is approximate. Every part says how it is known: drawn (traced from a plate or a survey map), written (placed from a written account) or approximate (inferred), and a part that says nothing is approximate. For Broad Street Station the rooms of the first floor, the hall's octagon, its benches and counters, the portico and the concourse's width are traced from the first-floor plan of 1919 (Plate 15), fitted by the 240-ft frontage; the levels, the dome and the basement are scaled from the sections (Plates 17 and 18), whose figures are illegible; the first floor's elevation, wall thicknesses, door heights, the basement's piers and use, the stairs to the platforms and the concourse's height are approximate. The sheet's numbered badges show which: solid for drawn, a ring for written, a dashed ring for approximate. Code: tools/stations/broad_street_plan.py, tools/cutaway/sheet.py.

St. Louis-San Francisco Ry., for the end of the segregated waiting rooms; and OpenStreetMap for the ground. Each is named on the sheet, and the wayfinding notes carry their own.

The sheet (cutaways/<name>.json, tools/cutaway/sheet.py) is 32 × 24 in at 300 dpi (9,600 × 7,200 pixels): the cutaway large, the flat section beneath it, and a column of numbered callouts, each anchored to a drawn part in either drawing, with the key, the sources and the notes. A badge that would hide the part it names is set off it into clear space, with a thin leader to a small dot on the part, drawn like the wayfinding labels' leaders. Callout text is 54 pixels (13 pt; 9.7 pt on the 24 × 18 in print), and the smallest text on the sheet, the two-digit numbers in the badges on the drawings, is 40 pixels (9.6 pt; 7.2 pt at 24 × 18). There are no canvas files: a canvas's 1.5 in of mirrored wrap would carry the title and the column round its sides. Its title block sits in the bottom-right corner, below the column.

Sets: a map framed by its places

tools/sets/set.py frames a big map with up to fourteen numbered boxes (the 1925 streetcars use one per stop), each 1,800 m of ground across, cut from the scene of its own year and rasterized from the vector at the pixels it fills, with a note written from the sources stored beside it. The same tool makes the map alone, a print per box (2,100 m across), canvas files with 1.5 in of mirrored wrap, and the box's page in the explorer. isoscape locate tells it where each box falls in the picture. A set is one file, sets/<name>.json; "Making a set" in the README walks through one.

What the series cannot show:

  • Survivorship. Only buildings standing today are measured. Downtown Richmond in 1900 had far more buildings than the 1900 scene, because most of them were torn down later. The scenes show what survives, plus the few curated losses.
  • Heaped years. Assessors often round: in this data, many buildings are dated to exactly 1900 or 1910. Read the decades loosely.
  • Today's ground. The elevation is today's lidar, so the I-95 cut through Shockoe Valley is there in 1737. Curated water is laid flat at the lowest ground beneath it.
  • The jump to today. Undated buildings appear only in today's scene, so the last step of a series adds many more buildings than really went up in that interval.

Code: src/history.ts; the history file is history/<series>.json; the tracing tools are in tools/sanborn/ (Python: NumPy, SciPy, OpenCV, Shapely), and their output is history/sanborn/.

Plates and the title block

Every print and canvas carries a small title block in its bottom-right corner, like the corner of a drafting sheet: the isoscape cube, the maker, the piece's place in the catalog (collection, set and piece), its plate number, version, the year it was drawn, the point the view was drawn around and the direction it faces. On a 36 × 24 in sheet it is 1,690 × 239 pixels at 300 dpi, about 5.6 × 0.8 in; smaller sheets scale it by the square root of their long side over 36 in (so 4.6 in across on a 24 × 18), which keeps it legible. Its right edge lines up with the art, and it sits as low as the art's side margin allows, with clear paper around it a third of its height wide. Where the foot of a sheet is full (the two set sheets) it sits in the header instead, and only if no place fits at full size is it shrunk, in steps of a tenth. On a canvas it goes on the face, never the wrap. A piece that is not a map can say what it is in one more line under the maker, at 28 units, about 7 pt on a 24 × 18 in sheet: the Broad Street cutaway's says "Building cutaway". The plates themselves are listed in catalog/plates.json.

Archive and explorer

Every build is kept under out/builds/<timestamp>-<name>/ as the SVG, a thumbnail and a meta.json. The meta file records:

  • the region's settings;
  • the camera bearing used;
  • every count the build measured (features, ground areas, buildings by height source, bridge spans, rapid cells, contour segments, strips);
  • the build's time and file size;
  • the git commit, its subject, and whether src/ or regions/ had uncommitted changes;
  • an optional --note saying what the build was trying;
  • for a dated scene, its year, the year before it in the series, and the curated features drawn in it, with their sources.

backfill builds an older commit's code against today's cached data, so the archive can include outputs from before it existed.

After every build, out/index.html is rewritten: this explorer. It is a single file with the build list and these pages embedded in it, so it opens from disk with no server. The pages are Markdown files in pages/. To add one, see the README.

Beside it go plain pages that need no script, for search engines and link previews: each of these pages as p/<slug>.html, each map as m/<region>.html (and each year of a dated series as m/<region>-<year>.html) with its picture and this same "How this view was made" text, and a sitemap.xml listing them all.

Chat apps and social sites never see the part of an address after #, so every view in the explorer would preview as the front page. Each map, each year of a series and each page also has a share page in s/, named as its plain page is, with its own title, description and picture, and a script that sends a visitor on into the explorer at that view. The Link button copies a share page's address, and the address bar shows it while you look, so an address copied from either previews as what you were looking at. The pictures are 1,200 × 630 pixels (1.91:1): a map cropped from its middle, saved as a JPEG at quality 86, about 140 to 260 KB; a page with a sheet of its own, such as a set or a cutaway, uses its drawing alone, not the whole sheet.

Code: src/archive.ts, src/explorer.ts, src/pages.ts, src/story.ts, src/static-pages.ts.

On a print, every line of the credit is at least 7 pt at the smallest size that print is sold at: a 24 × 36 in file is also printed at 8 × 12 in, a third of its size, so there the credit is set at 21 pt (88 px at 300 dpi) and wraps onto as many lines as it needs, between the title and the title block. Where a long subtitle leaves too little room there (Gwynn's Island), the credit runs instead on wide lines from the end of the title to the right edge of the map, above the corner where the title block goes; the map itself is not moved or cropped.
