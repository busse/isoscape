# Artist statement

I have been fascinated by maps and data visualization for as long as I can remember. A map is a set of decisions about what matters: what gets drawn, what gets left off, what gets a name. Those decisions are an argument about a place, and I have never stopped wanting to read them.

That fascination runs straight through my photography. I think about where a picture was taken almost as much as what is in it, and my ties to a place shape what I notice and what I come back to. Maps have their own taxonomies (roads ranked by size, buildings by use, water by kind), and they feed the taxonomies of everything else. Where an image was taken is part of how it is cataloged, found and remembered.

isoscape grew out of that. It turns open map data into small isometric drawings: building outlines, streets, rivers and elevation, plus, for Richmond, the outlines traced from historic fire-insurance maps. They are sketches, not surveys. What they offer is a whole neighborhood at a glance, and a way to watch it change.

I built isoscape with AI, working with Claude in ordinary chat windows. What interests me about these tools isn't that they make things for us. It's that a person can build a system around them: rules, constraints, checks, a style, and a clear idea of what the result is for. The tool then works inside that frame. I talked about this at AI Ready RVA in October 2026, in [Humans, Art, Tools, and AI](https://github.com/busse/talk-2610-airva). A tool can't mean anything on its own. The person who sets the constraints and chooses what to keep is the one making the work. isoscape is that idea put into practice, and because the code is free and open, you can set your own constraints and draw your own places.

These maps are a beginning. They prepare the ground for several creative projects in 2027 that explore images, history, place, taxonomy, cataloging and change, and how cutting-edge tools, AI among them, can help explore the seams and boundaries where experience, memory and the way we tell stories meet.

— Chris Busse, Richmond, Virginia
