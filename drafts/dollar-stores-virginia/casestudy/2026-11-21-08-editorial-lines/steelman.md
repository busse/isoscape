# Editorial lines and data viz, steelmanned (November 21, 15:41)

Chris, 15:41: "in chat, steelman possible editorial lines and data viz". Written with Claude.
The numbers are from steps 5 to 7.

## 1. The road in

- **The line.** In a Virginia town, the edge-of-town Dollar General is on the road most
  people drive in on, near a junction, usually just inside the town line.
- **Strongest case.**
  - It answers Chris's question directly, and the key test was written before the data.
  - 13 of 18 town edge stores are on one of the two busiest roads in, against 47 percent of
    other edge sites.
  - 33 percent are within 250 m of a junction of two state or US routes, against 7 percent.
  - 39 percent are outside the limits, against 82 percent.
  - The roads were already busy when the stores came.
- **Viz.** The heat-map squares, one per store, arranged as a grid. Each shows the dark road
  meeting the gray town, with the yellow square at the seam.
- **Weak spot.** 18 stores. Convenience stores and supermarkets also sit on busy roads, so
  "busy road" alone is ordinary retail.

## 2. It's the drive, not the map

- **The line.** Most Dollar General stores are in town. The edge feeling comes from the drive.
- **Strongest case.**
  - 264 of the 359 stores near a free-standing town are in town.
  - The edge band holds 11 percent of them, against 25 percent of random main-road frontage.
  - The ones at the edge are on the busiest road in, so nearly everyone passes them.
  - That explains the feeling without claiming a pattern that isn't there.
- **Viz.** A "drive in" strip for a few towns: a straight-line diagram from the county line to
  the courthouse along the busiest road, with every store passed marked in order. Beside it,
  the pooled chart showing that the edge is not crowded.
- **Weak spot.** It deflates the question. That is honest, but a poster needs a picture of
  something that is there.

## 3. Old in town, new in between

- **The line.** The chain's older stores sit in towns. Its newer ones fill the country and
  the approaches between them.
- **Strongest case.**
  - 46 percent of in-town Dollar General stores were authorized after 2005, against 74
    percent of edge stores.
  - In the editorial 20, all five stores in no Census place came 2016 to 2024.
  - Mathews and Middlesex show it cleanly: three village stores from 2004-07, four newer
    ones on the state routes between them.
- **Viz.** The same grid, with marks tinted by era. Or one county (Mathews and Middlesex)
  drawn three times: 2005, 2015, 2025.
- **Weak spot.**
  - SNAP authorization is not an opening date.
  - "By 2005" hides everything older.
  - Statewide between-places dating was not tested formally.

## 4. Where the grocery isn't

- **The line.** The newer stores serve people far from a supermarket.
- **Strongest case.** In Mathews and Middlesex the four newer stores are 4.8 to 12.8 km by
  road from a grocery. This links to the research literature on food access.
- **Viz.** The catchment map: each store's area, its population center, and the distance to
  the nearest grocery.
- **Weak spot.**
  - It was tested in one region only.
  - Statewide, edge stores are not on the far side of town from the supermarket (18 percent,
    against 25 percent of random sites).
  - The subject is policy-charged, so it needs the researchers' and the company's positions
    side by side, with no verdict.

## 5. Two kinds of edge

- **The line.** A town's edge and a city's edge are different places.
- **Strongest case.**
  - Towns: 13 of 18 edge stores are on a top-two road in.
  - Cities: 1 of 16.
  - It keeps the reader from reading a suburban strip as "edge of town".
- **Viz.** Paired panels: a town's squares beside a city's.
- **Weak spot.** It is a supporting point rather than a headline.

## 6. The stores trace the traffic (the artistic version)

- **The line.** Draw Virginia's roads by traffic, put every store on them, and the stores sit
  where the traffic runs.
- **Strongest case.**
  - It is a picture before it is an argument, and it fits the dense, reduced artistic version.
  - The data is public domain.
- **Viz.** Statewide roads in the blue ramp with yellow dots, or the 227 tiles each reduced to
  limits, edge ring, top-traffic road and stores.
- **Weak spot.** All retail follows traffic. Without a comparison it says "stores are on
  roads".

## Suggested combination

- **Editorial poster.**
  - Line 1 as the headline and grid.
  - Line 2 as the honest kicker in the "Is it true?" panel.
  - Line 3 as one era chart.
  - Line 5 as a footnote panel.
  - Line 4 only with both sides cited.
- **Artistic version.** Line 6.
